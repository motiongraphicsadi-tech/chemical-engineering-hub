# Introduction

Basic feedback control is widely used in industry, but many industrial processes require more sophisticated control techniques.

Advanced control strategies are designed to:

- Improve disturbance rejection
- Increase control accuracy
- Improve process stability
- Reduce operating costs
- Enhance product quality

Common advanced control strategies include:

- Feed Forward Control
- Cascade Control
- Ratio Control
- Split Range Control
- Selective Control

These strategies are extensively used in modern process industries.

---

# Why It Matters

Industrial processes often experience:

- Large disturbances
- Process interactions
- Dead time
- Variable operating conditions

Conventional feedback control may not always provide satisfactory performance.

Advanced control strategies help:

- Improve dynamic response
- Reduce variability
- Improve safety
- Increase production efficiency

Many critical industrial units rely on advanced control techniques.

---

# Core Concept

The objective of advanced control is to improve process performance beyond what can be achieved using simple feedback loops.

Instead of relying solely on:

$$
Error = SP - PV
$$

advanced strategies may use:

- Disturbance measurements
- Multiple controllers
- Multiple manipulated variables
- Process relationships

to improve control quality.

---

# Working Principle

Advanced control systems generally operate by:

1. Monitoring process conditions.
2. Detecting disturbances.
3. Predicting process behavior.
4. Applying corrective action.
5. Maintaining desired operating conditions.

The control action may occur before the disturbance affects the process.

This improves overall performance.

---

# Mathematical Definition

Most advanced control systems are based on process transfer functions.

General process model:

$$
G(s)
=
\frac{Output}
{Input}
$$

Controller design attempts to compensate for process dynamics and disturbances.

Advanced control often combines multiple transfer functions and control loops.

---

# Feed Forward Control

Feed Forward Control acts before the disturbance affects the process.

Unlike feedback control, feed forward control measures disturbances directly.

Basic concept:

$$
Disturbance \rightarrow Controller \rightarrow Corrective\ Action
$$

The controller predicts the effect of the disturbance and compensates immediately.

---

## Feed Forward Control Strategy

Steps:

1. Measure disturbance.
2. Predict process effect.
3. Calculate correction.
4. Apply control action.

Applications:

- Fuel-air ratio control
- Heat exchanger control
- Flow control systems

Feed forward control improves disturbance rejection significantly.

---

# Cascade Control

Cascade control uses two controllers arranged in series.

Structure:

- Primary Controller
- Secondary Controller

The output of the primary controller becomes the setpoint of the secondary controller.

Benefits:

- Faster disturbance rejection
- Improved control accuracy
- Better stability

Cascade control is widely used in temperature and flow control systems.

---

## Cascade Control Strategy

Typical structure:

$$
Master\ Controller
\rightarrow
Slave\ Controller
\rightarrow
Process
$$

The secondary loop responds to disturbances before they affect the primary process variable.

---

# Ratio Control

Ratio control maintains a fixed relationship between two process variables.

Basic equation:

$$
Ratio
=
\frac{Flow_1}
{Flow_2}
$$

Desired condition:

$$
\frac{Flow_1}
{Flow_2}
=
Constant
$$

Applications:

- Fuel-air mixing
- Chemical blending
- Feed composition control

Ratio control ensures proper material proportions.

---

# Split Range Control

Split range control uses a single controller to manipulate multiple final control elements.

One controller output controls:

- Multiple valves
- Multiple utilities
- Multiple actuators

Example:

Temperature control using:

- Cooling water valve
- Steam valve

Low controller output may open cooling water.

High controller output may open steam.

This allows one controller to manage different operating conditions.

---

# Selective Control

Selective control chooses the most important process variable and uses it for control action.

Common examples:

- High selector
- Low selector

Mathematically:

$$
Output
=
Max(x_1,x_2)
$$

or

$$
Output
=
Min(x_1,x_2)
$$

Applications:

- Equipment protection
- Safety systems
- Compressor control

Selective control helps prevent unsafe operating conditions.

---

# Feedforward vs Feedback Control

| Feature | Feed Forward | Feedback |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Disturbance Measurement | Required | Not Required |
| Correction Timing | Before Disturbance Effect | After Disturbance Effect |
| Response Speed | Faster | Slower |
| Accuracy | Depends on Model | Depends on Feedback |
| Complexity | Higher | Lower |

In practice, feed forward and feedback are often combined.

This provides superior control performance.

# Engineering Perspective

Modern DCS and PLC systems support advanced control strategies as standard features.

Engineers typically apply:

- Feed Forward Control for measurable disturbances
- Cascade Control for secondary disturbances
- Ratio Control for blending systems
- Split Range Control for utility management
- Selective Control for equipment protection

These strategies are commonly encountered in industrial process plants.

# Solved Example

A blending process requires:

$$
Flow_A = 100\;kg/hr
$$

Desired ratio:

$$
\frac{Flow_B}
{Flow_A}
=
2
$$

Therefore:

$$
Flow_B
=
2(100)
$$

$$
Flow_B
=
200\;kg/hr
$$

The ratio controller adjusts Flow B to maintain the desired ratio.

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Confuse feed forward and feedback control
- Assume cascade control uses only one controller
- Ignore disturbance measurements
- Misunderstand ratio control objectives
- Forget selector logic in selective control

<WarningBox>
Feed Forward Control cannot eliminate disturbances effectively unless the disturbance can be measured accurately.
</WarningBox>

- Split Range Control uses one controller to operate multiple final control elements.
- Selective Control prioritizes critical process variables.
