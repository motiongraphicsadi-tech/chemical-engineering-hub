# Properties of Radiation

<TopicRoadmap />


## What Happens When Radiation Hits a Surface?

When thermal radiation strikes a surface, the incoming energy does not necessarily remain entirely within the material.

Instead, the radiation can undergo three possible interactions:

```text
Incoming Radiation
        ↓

     Surface

    ↙   ↓   ↘

Reflected
Absorbed
Transmitted
```

Therefore, every incident radiation beam is divided into:

- Absorbed Radiation
- Reflected Radiation
- Transmitted Radiation

---

# Absorbed Radiation

Absorbed radiation enters the material and increases its internal energy.

The fraction absorbed is called **Absorptivity**.

$$
\alpha
$$

Where:

- \(\alpha\) = Absorptivity

Examples:

- Black paint has high absorptivity.
- A black body has maximum absorptivity.

---

# Reflected Radiation

Reflected radiation bounces away from the surface.

The fraction reflected is called **Reflectivity**.

$$
\rho
$$

Where:

- \(\rho\) = Reflectivity

Examples:

- Polished aluminum
- Mirrors
- Shiny metal surfaces

These materials reflect a large portion of incident radiation.

---

# Transmitted Radiation

Transmitted radiation passes through the material.

The fraction transmitted is called **Transmissivity**.

$$
\tau
$$

Where:

- \(\tau\) = Transmissivity

Examples:

- Glass
- Transparent plastics
- Certain optical materials

These materials allow radiation to pass through them.

---

# Radiation Balance

Energy cannot disappear.

Therefore, all incoming radiation must be:

- Absorbed
- Reflected
- Transmitted

This gives the radiation balance equation:

$$
\alpha + \rho + \tau = 1
$$

Where:

| Symbol | Property |
|----------|----------|
| \(\alpha\) | Absorptivity |
| \(\rho\) | Reflectivity |
| \(\tau\) | Transmissivity |

This equation is one of the most important relationships in radiation heat transfer.

---

# Special Cases

## Black Body

A black body absorbs all incoming radiation.

$$
\alpha = 1
$$

$$
\rho = 0
$$

$$
\tau = 0
$$

---

## Perfect Reflector

A perfect reflector reflects all incoming radiation.

$$
\rho = 1
$$

$$
\alpha = 0
$$

$$
\tau = 0
$$

---

## Transparent Material

A perfectly transparent material transmits all incoming radiation.

$$
\tau = 1
$$

$$
\alpha = 0
$$

$$
\rho = 0
$$

# Why These Properties Matter

Surface properties determine:

- Radiation heat loss
- Solar energy absorption
- Furnace performance
- Insulation effectiveness
- Thermal imaging behavior

Choosing the correct surface finish can significantly affect heat transfer performance.

---

# Engineering Perspective

When a process engineer sees a surface, they should ask:

- Does it absorb radiation?
- Does it reflect radiation?
- Does it transmit radiation?

The answer determines how that surface interacts with thermal energy.

For example:

- Black surfaces absorb more heat.
- Shiny surfaces reflect more heat.
- Transparent surfaces transmit radiation.

# Key Takeaways

- Radiation striking a surface can be absorbed, reflected, or transmitted.
- Absorptivity is represented by \(\alpha\).
- Reflectivity is represented by \(\rho\).
- Transmissivity is represented by \(\tau\).
- Radiation energy balance is:

$$
\alpha + \rho + \tau = 1
$$

- Surface properties strongly influence radiation heat transfer.

---

# Summary

When thermal radiation strikes a surface, part of it is absorbed, part is reflected, and part may be transmitted. These interactions are described by absorptivity (\(\alpha\)), reflectivity (\(\rho\)), and transmissivity (\(\tau\)). Together they satisfy the radiation balance equation \(\alpha + \rho + \tau = 1\). Understanding these properties is essential for analyzing radiation heat transfer and selecting suitable engineering materials.
