# Introduction

Viscosity is one of the most important fluid properties in fluid mechanics.

It represents the internal resistance offered by a fluid to flow.

When fluid layers move relative to one another, frictional forces develop between adjacent layers.

These forces are caused by viscosity.

Examples:

- Honey flows slowly because it has high viscosity.
- Water flows easily because it has low viscosity.
- Air has very low viscosity compared to liquids.

Viscosity plays a major role in:

- Pipe flow
- Pump design
- Mixing operations
- Heat transfer systems

Understanding viscosity is essential for analyzing fluid flow in chemical engineering.

---

# Why It Matters

Every industrial fluid system experiences flow resistance.

Examples include:

- Pipelines
- Heat exchangers
- Distillation columns
- Reactors
- Pumps

Viscosity affects:

- Pressure drop
- Pumping power
- Flow regime
- Mixing efficiency
- Energy consumption

Accurate viscosity data is critical for process design and operation.

---

# Definition

Viscosity is the property of a fluid that resists relative motion between adjacent fluid layers.

It is often referred to as the internal friction of a fluid.

A highly viscous fluid:

- Flows slowly
- Offers greater resistance

A low-viscosity fluid:

- Flows easily
- Offers less resistance

Viscosity is a fundamental transport property of fluids.

---

# Physical Significance

Viscosity explains why energy is lost during fluid flow.

As fluid layers move:

- Molecular interactions occur
- Frictional forces develop
- Energy is dissipated

This resistance influences:

- Pressure losses
- Pumping requirements
- Flow behavior

Without viscosity, real fluid flow behavior could not be accurately predicted.

# Mathematical Definition

Viscosity relates shear stress to velocity gradient.

For Newtonian fluids:

$$
\tau
=
\mu
\frac{du}{dy}
$$

Where:

- \(\tau\) = Shear Stress
- \(\mu\) = Dynamic Viscosity
- \(du/dy\) = Velocity Gradient

This equation is known as Newton's Law of Viscosity.

---

# Dynamic Viscosity

Dynamic viscosity measures resistance to fluid deformation under shear stress.

Symbol:

$$
\mu
$$

Unit:

$$
Pa\cdot s
$$

or

$$
N\cdot s/m^2
$$

Typical values:

| Fluid | Dynamic Viscosity |
|---------|---------|
| Water (20°C) | 0.001 Pa·s |
| Air (20°C) | 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s |
| Glycerin | 1.5 Pa·s |

Higher dynamic viscosity means greater resistance to flow.

---

# Newton's Law of Viscosity

Newton's Law states:

The shear stress in a fluid is directly proportional to the velocity gradient.

Mathematically:

$$
\tau
=
\mu
\frac{du}{dy}
$$

This relationship applies to Newtonian fluids such as:

- Water
- Air
- Alcohol
- Most gases

The law forms the foundation of fluid flow analysis.

---

# Velocity Gradient

The velocity gradient represents the rate of change of velocity between adjacent fluid layers.

Mathematically:

$$
\frac{du}{dy}
$$

Where:

- \(u\) = Fluid Velocity
- \(y\) = Distance Perpendicular to Flow

Unit:

$$
s^{-1}
$$

A larger velocity gradient produces greater shear stress.

Velocity gradients are especially important near solid boundaries.

---

# Kinematic Viscosity

Kinematic viscosity is the ratio of dynamic viscosity to density.

Mathematically:

$$
\nu
=
\frac{\mu}{\rho}
$$

Where:

- \(\nu\) = Kinematic Viscosity
- \(\mu\) = Dynamic Viscosity
- \(\rho\) = Density

Unit:

$$
m^2/s
$$

Kinematic viscosity is frequently used in fluid flow calculations and Reynolds number analysis.

---

# Temperature Effects on Viscosity

Temperature significantly affects viscosity.

However, the effect differs for liquids and gases.

---

## Liquids

For liquids:

As temperature increases:

$$
\mu \downarrow
$$

Viscosity decreases.

Reason:

- Molecular attraction weakens.
- Fluid layers move more easily.

Examples:

- Hot oil flows more easily than cold oil.
- Warm water flows more easily than cold water.

---

## Gases

For gases:

As temperature increases:

$$
\mu \uparrow
$$

Viscosity increases.

Reason:

- Molecular collisions become more frequent.
- Momentum transfer increases.

This behavior is opposite to liquids.

---

# Viscosity of Liquids and Gases

### Liquids

Characteristics:

- High viscosity
- Strong molecular attraction
- Viscosity decreases with temperature

Examples:

- Water
- Oil
- Glycerin

---

### Gases

Characteristics:

- Low viscosity
- Weak molecular attraction
- Viscosity increases with temperature

Examples:

- Air
- Steam
- Nitrogen

Understanding these differences is important in industrial process calculations.

# Factors Affecting It

Several factors influence viscosity.

### Temperature

Most important factor.

### Pressure

Significant for liquids at high pressures.

### Molecular Structure

Large molecules generally have higher viscosity.

### Concentration

Solutions become more viscous as concentration increases.

### Suspended Particles

Slurries often exhibit high viscosity.

These factors must be considered during process design.

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Confuse dynamic and kinematic viscosity
- Forget unit conversions
- Assume viscosity is constant
- Ignore temperature effects
- Confuse viscosity with density

<WarningBox>
Density and viscosity are different properties. Density measures mass per unit volume, while viscosity measures resistance to flow.
</WarningBox>

# Key Takeaways

- Viscosity measures resistance to fluid flow.
- Dynamic viscosity is represented by \(\mu\).
- Kinematic viscosity is represented by \(\nu\).
- Newton's Law of Viscosity relates shear stress to velocity gradient.
- Velocity gradients create shear stress.
- Liquid viscosity decreases with temperature.
- Gas viscosity increases with temperature.
- Viscosity strongly influences industrial fluid flow behavior.
