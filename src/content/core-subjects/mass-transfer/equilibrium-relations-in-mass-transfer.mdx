# Equilibrium Relations in Mass Transfer Introduction

Mass transfer operations such as absorption, distillation, extraction, humidification, and stripping involve the transfer of components between different phases.

The maximum extent of transfer is governed by **phase equilibrium**. Equilibrium relations describe how a component distributes itself between phases when no net mass transfer occurs.

Understanding equilibrium relationships is essential because they determine:

- Driving force for mass transfer
- Maximum achievable separation
- Equipment size requirements
- Process efficiency

Equilibrium concepts form the foundation of almost all separation processes in chemical engineering.

---

# Why It Matters

Equilibrium relations help engineers:

- Design absorbers and strippers
- Analyze distillation columns
- Perform extraction calculations
- Determine separation feasibility
- Calculate driving forces

Industrial applications include:

- Gas absorption
- Distillation
- Solvent extraction
- Drying operations
- Environmental control systems

Without equilibrium data, accurate mass transfer design is impossible.

---

# Core Concept

When two phases are brought into contact, mass transfer occurs until equilibrium is reached.

At equilibrium:

$$
\text{Rate of transfer from Phase 1 to Phase 2}
=
\text{Rate of transfer from Phase 2 to Phase 1}
$$

Net transfer becomes zero.

Although molecules continue moving between phases, no overall concentration change occurs.

Equilibrium relations mathematically describe this state.

---

# Classification and Types

## Gas-Liquid Equilibrium

Occurs between vapor and liquid phases.

Examples:

- CO₂ absorption in water
- Distillation systems

---

## Liquid-Liquid Equilibrium

Occurs between two immiscible liquids.

Examples:

- Solvent extraction

---

## Solid-Fluid Equilibrium

Occurs in adsorption and drying systems.

---

## Vapor-Liquid Equilibrium (VLE)

Most important equilibrium relationship in chemical engineering.

Used extensively in:

- Distillation
- Flash calculations
- Separation design

---

# Working Principle

The equilibrium process occurs as follows:

1. Two phases come into contact.
2. Concentration difference creates a driving force.
3. Mass transfer begins.
4. Component distributes between phases.
5. Equilibrium concentrations are established.
6. Net mass transfer becomes zero.

At equilibrium:

$$
\Delta \mu = 0
$$

where \(\mu\) represents chemical potential.

---

# Mathematical Definition

Equilibrium relationships connect concentrations in different phases.

General form:

$$
y^*=f(x)
$$

Where:

- \(y^*\) = Equilibrium gas-phase composition
- \(x\) = Liquid-phase composition

This relationship forms the basis of equilibrium diagrams.

# Derivation and Theory

# Phase Equilibrium

At equilibrium:

$$
\mu_A^{(gas)}
=
\mu_A^{(liquid)}
$$

The chemical potential of a component must be equal in both phases.

This condition leads to equilibrium relationships such as Henry's Law and Raoult's Law.

---

# Henry's Law

Henry's Law applies to dilute solutions.

It states:

$$
P_A
=
H x_A
$$

Where:

| Symbol | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| \(P_A\) | Partial Pressure |
| \(H\) | Henry's Constant |
| \(x_A\) | Liquid Mole Fraction |

---

## Physical Meaning

Higher Henry's constant means:

- Lower solubility
- More volatile component

Lower Henry's constant means:

- Higher solubility

---

# Raoult's Law

Applicable to ideal liquid mixtures.

It states:

$$
P_A
=
x_A P_A^{sat}
$$

Where:

- \(P_A^{sat}\) = Saturation pressure

For total pressure:

$$
P
=
\sum x_i P_i^{sat}
$$

Raoult's Law forms the basis of vapor-liquid equilibrium calculations.

---

# Distribution Coefficient

The distribution coefficient describes equilibrium partitioning between phases.

Definition:

$$
m
=
\frac{y^*}{x}
$$

Where:

- \(m\) = Distribution coefficient

For Henry's Law:

$$
m
=
\frac{H}{P}
$$

The distribution coefficient determines how easily a component transfers between phases.

---

# Equilibrium Diagrams

Equilibrium diagrams graphically represent equilibrium relationships.

General form:

$$
y^*
=
f(x)
$$

The equilibrium curve shows allowable equilibrium compositions.

These diagrams are extensively used in:

- Absorption calculations
- Distillation design
- Extraction systems

---

# Operating Line Concept

The operating line represents actual process conditions.

General form:

$$
y
=
mx+c
$$

The difference between:

- Operating line
- Equilibrium line

provides the mass transfer driving force.

Larger separation:

$$
(y-y^*)
$$

means larger transfer rates.

# Solved Example

### Problem

A dilute gas is absorbed into a liquid.

Given:

$$
H=500 \; atm
$$

Liquid mole fraction:

$$
x_A=0.002
$$

Calculate equilibrium partial pressure.

### Given

- \(H=500\;atm\)
- \(x_A=0.002\)

### Solution

Using Henry's Law:

$$
P_A=Hx_A
$$

Substituting:

$$
P_A=(500)(0.002)
$$

$$
P_A=1.0\;atm
$$

### Final Answer

$$
P_A=1.0\;atm
$$

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing equilibrium and operating lines
- Using Henry's Law outside dilute conditions
- Applying Raoult's Law to highly non-ideal systems
- Ignoring temperature effects
- Mixing mole fractions and concentrations

# Summary

Equilibrium relations describe how components distribute themselves between phases when thermodynamic equilibrium is reached. Henry's Law, Raoult's Law, and distribution coefficients provide mathematical relationships that connect compositions in different phases.

These equilibrium relationships form the foundation of absorption, distillation, extraction, humidification, and many other separation processes. Understanding equilibrium diagrams and operating lines is essential for mass transfer analysis, equipment design, and industrial process optimization.
