# Diffusion Fundamentals Introduction

Diffusion is the most fundamental mechanism of mass transfer and forms the basis of many chemical engineering operations.

It is a natural process in which molecules move from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration because of their random molecular motion.

Examples of diffusion include:

- Perfume spreading through a room
- Oxygen dissolving into water
- Sugar dissolving in tea
- Gas transfer in absorption towers
- Moisture removal during drying

Diffusion occurs in:

- Gases
- Liquids
- Solids

Understanding diffusion is essential because most mass transfer operations involve molecular transport either alone or combined with fluid motion.

# Core Concept

Diffusion occurs because molecules possess continuous random motion.

When a concentration difference exists:

- More molecules move away from the high concentration region.
- Fewer molecules move toward the high concentration region.

This creates a net movement of matter.

The driving force for diffusion is:

$$
\text{Concentration Gradient}
$$

The larger the concentration gradient, the higher the diffusion rate.

---

## Important Terminology

### Diffusion

Movement of species due to molecular motion.

### Diffusivity

Property that indicates how rapidly diffusion occurs.

### Flux

Rate of mass transfer per unit area.

### Concentration Gradient

Change in concentration with distance.

### Diffusing Species

Component undergoing transfer.

### Stagnant Medium

A medium that does not move as a whole.

# Classification and Types

Diffusion can be classified into several categories.

## Molecular Diffusion

Occurs solely because of molecular motion.

Examples:

- Gas diffusion
- Solute diffusion in liquids

---

## Equimolar Counter Diffusion

Two species diffuse in opposite directions at equal molar rates.

Example:

- Diffusion of gases through a tube.

---

## Diffusion Through Stagnant Medium

One component diffuses through another non-moving component.

Example:

- Evaporation of water through stagnant air.

---

## Diffusion in Gases

Occurs rapidly because molecules move freely.

---

## Diffusion in Liquids

Occurs more slowly because molecules are closer together.

---

## Diffusion in Solids

Occurs very slowly because molecular mobility is limited.

---

# Working Principle

Diffusion occurs through the following sequence:

1. A concentration difference develops.
2. Molecules move randomly in all directions.
3. More molecules leave the high concentration region.
4. Net transfer occurs toward the low concentration region.
5. Concentration gradually becomes uniform.
6. Equilibrium is eventually reached.

At equilibrium:

$$
\frac{dC}{dz}=0
$$

and no net diffusion occurs.

---

# Mathematical Definition

The fundamental diffusion equation is Fick's First Law.

$$
N_A
=
-D_{AB}
\frac{dC_A}{dz}
$$

Where:

| Symbol | Description | Unit |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| $N_A$ | Diffusion Flux | mol/m²·s |
| $D_{AB}$ | Diffusivity | m²/s |
| $C_A$ | Concentration of A | mol/m³ |
| $z$ | Diffusion Direction | m |

The negative sign indicates diffusion occurs from high concentration to low concentration.

For simple diffusion analysis:

- Constant diffusivity

- One-dimensional diffusion
- Homogeneous medium
- Steady-state conditions

These assumptions simplify mathematical treatment.

# Derivation and Theory

Experimental observations show that diffusion flux is proportional to concentration gradient.

Therefore:

$$
N_A
\propto
-\frac{dC_A}{dz}
$$

Introducing diffusivity:

$$
N_A
=
-D_{AB}
\frac{dC_A}{dz}
$$

This relationship is known as Fick's First Law.

For transient diffusion:

$$
\frac{\partial C_A}{\partial t}
=
D_{AB}
\frac{\partial^2 C_A}{\partial z^2}
$$

This is Fick's Second Law.

It describes concentration variation with both time and position.

## Diffusion Through Stagnant Medium

For species A diffusing through stagnant B:

$$
N_A
=
\frac{D_{AB}C}{z_2-z_1}
\ln
\left(
\frac{1-y_{A2}}
{1-y_{A1}}
\right)
$$

This equation is important in gas-phase diffusion calculations.

# Graphical Interpretation

A typical diffusion profile shows:

- Concentration on Y-axis
- Distance on X-axis

Characteristics:

- High concentration at one boundary
- Low concentration at the other boundary
- Continuous concentration decrease across the medium

The slope of the concentration curve:

$$
\frac{dC_A}{dz}
$$

represents the driving force for diffusion.

Steeper slopes produce higher diffusion rates.

# Solved Example

### Problem

Oxygen diffuses through air.

Given:

$$
D_{AB}
=
2\times10^{-5}
\;m^2/s
$$

Concentration gradient:

$$
\frac{dC_A}{dz}
=
-1500
\;mol/m^4
$$

Calculate diffusion flux.

### Given

- \(D_{AB}=2\times10^{-5}\;m^2/s\)
- \(dC_A/dz=-1500\;mol/m^4\)

### Solution

Using Fick's First Law:

$$
N_A
=
-D_{AB}
\frac{dC_A}{dz}
$$

Substituting values:

$$
N_A
=
-(2\times10^{-5})
(-1500)
$$

$$
N_A
=
0.03
\;mol/m^2.s
$$

### Final Answer

$$
N_A
=
0.03
\;mol/m^2.s
$$

# Common Mistakes

- Ignoring the negative sign in Fick's Law
- Confusing diffusion and convection
- Using incorrect diffusivity units
- Forgetting concentration gradients
- Applying gas diffusivity values to liquids
- Ignoring stagnant-film effects

# Key Takeaways

- Diffusion is mass transfer caused by molecular motion.
- Concentration gradient is the driving force.
- Fick's First Law governs steady-state diffusion.
- Fick's Second Law describes transient diffusion.
- Diffusion occurs in gases, liquids, and solids.
- Gas diffusion is faster than liquid diffusion.
- Equimolar counter diffusion involves equal and opposite molar fluxes.
- Diffusion is fundamental to separation processes.
