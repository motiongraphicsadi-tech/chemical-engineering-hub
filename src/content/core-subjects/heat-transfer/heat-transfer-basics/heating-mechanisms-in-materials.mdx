# Heating mechanism in materials

Thermal conductivity tells us how easily heat flows through a material.

But **why does heat flow more easily through copper than wood?**

Why does air act as an insulator?

Why is water a better conductor than air?

To answer these questions, we must understand the microscopic mechanisms responsible for heat transfer in different materials.

# Heat Conduction in Metals

## Physical Mechanism

Metals contain a large number of free electrons.

These electrons move throughout the metal structure.

When one side of the metal is heated:

- Electrons gain kinetic energy.
- Electrons move rapidly.
- Energy is transported to cooler regions.

This makes heat transfer extremely efficient.

  title=""
  image="/images/core-subject/heat-transfer/heating-mechanisms-in-materials/in-metals.webp"

---

## Mathematical Interpretation

According to Fourier's Law:

$$
q=-k\frac{dT}{dx}
$$

Metals possess large values of:

$$
k
$$

therefore:

$$
q \uparrow
$$

Heat transfer becomes very high.

---

## Examples

| Material | Thermal Conductivity (W/m·K) |
|-----------|-----------|
| Silver | 430 |
| Copper | 400 |
| Aluminum | 237 |
| Steel | 50 |

# Heat Conduction in Non-Metals

## Physical Mechanism

Non-metals do not contain free electrons.

Heat is transferred through:

- Atomic vibrations
- Molecular vibrations
- Crystal lattice vibrations

These vibrations are often called:

> Phonons


<DiagramBox
  title=""
  image="/images/core-subject/heat-transfer/heating-mechanisms-in-materials/in-nonmetals.webp"
/>

---

## Why Conductivity is Lower

Since atoms remain fixed in position:

- Energy transfer is slower.
- No free electrons are available.

Thus:

$$
k_{non-metal}
<
k_{metal}
$$

---

## Examples

| Material | Thermal Conductivity (W/m·K) |
|-----------|-----------|
| Glass | 1 |
| Brick | 0.7 |
| Wood | 0.12 |

---

# Heat Transfer in Liquids

## Physical Mechanism

In liquids:

- Molecules are mobile ( but not free like gases ).
- Free electrons are absent.

Heat transfer occurs through:

- Molecular interaction
- Molecular diffusion
- Small-scale fluid motion


<DiagramBox
  title=""
  image="/images/core-subject/heat-transfer/heating-mechanisms-in-materials/in-liquid.webp"
/>
---

## Important Observation

Although conduction occurs,

heat transfer in liquids is often dominated by:

### Convection

because fluid particles can move ( but not freely like gases ).

Therefore:

In practical systems:

$$
q_{convection}
>
q_{conduction}
$$

---

## Examples

| Liquid | Thermal Conductivity (W/m·K) |
|-----------|-----------|
| Water | 0.6 |
| Ethanol | 0.17 |
| Oil | 0.13 |

# Heat Transfer in Gases

## Physical Mechanism

Unlike solids and liquids, gas molecules are separated by relatively large distances. Therefore, gases cannot transfer heat through free electrons or lattice vibrations.

Heat transfer in gases occurs primarily through:

- Random molecular motion
- Molecular collisions
- Exchange of kinetic energy between molecules

When a gas is heated, molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster. These energetic molecules collide with neighboring molecules and transfer part of their energy. Through millions of such collisions, thermal energy gradually moves from the hotter region to the colder region.

This mechanism is explained by the **Kinetic Theory of Gases**.

## Kinetic Theory of Gases

According to kinetic theory, the average kinetic energy of a gas molecule is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.

$$
KE_{avg}=\frac{3}{2}k_BT
$$

Where:

- $KE_{avg}$ = Average Kinetic Energy per Molecule
- $k_B$ = Boltzmann Constant
- $T$ = Absolute Temperature (K)

This equation shows that increasing temperature increases molecular kinetic energy.

---

## Molecular Velocity and Heat Transfer

The root-mean-square velocity of gas molecules is:

$$
v_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}
$$

Where:

- $v_{rms}$ = Root Mean Square Molecular Velocity (m/s)
- $R$ = Universal Gas Constant ($8.314\;J/mol\cdot K$)
- $T$ = Absolute Temperature (K)
- $M$ = Molecular Weight (kg/kmol)

From the equation:

$$
v_{rms} \propto \sqrt{T}
$$

As temperature increases:

- Molecular velocity increases
- Collision frequency increases
- Energy transferred per collision increases

Therefore heat transfer through the gas becomes more effective.

---

## Temperature Dependence of Thermal Conductivity

For most gases:

$$
k \propto T^n
$$

where:

$$
n \approx 0.5 - 1
$$

This means thermal conductivity generally increases with temperature.

Unlike metals, whose thermal conductivity often decreases with temperature, gases become better heat conductors at higher temperatures because molecular motion becomes more energetic.

---

## Why Gases Have Low Thermal Conductivity

Although gas molecules move rapidly, gases remain poor conductors of heat because:

- Molecules are widely spaced.
- Energy must travel through intermittent collisions.
- No free electrons are available to transport energy.

As a result:

$$
k_{gas}
<
k_{liquid}
<
k_{solid}
$$

This explains why air is commonly used as an insulating medium in buildings, refrigerators, and industrial insulation systems.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Metals conduct heat primarily through free electrons.
- Non-metals conduct heat through lattice vibrations (phonons).
- Liquids transfer heat through molecular interaction and often convection.
- Gases transfer heat through molecular collisions explained by kinetic theory.
- Metals have the highest thermal conductivity.
- Gases generally have the lowest thermal conductivity.
- Understanding conduction mechanisms is essential for material selection and heat transfer design.

# Summary

Heat conduction occurs through different microscopic mechanisms depending on the state and structure of the material. In metals, thermal energy is transported primarily by free electrons, resulting in very high thermal conductivity. In non-metals, heat transfer occurs through lattice vibrations (phonons), making conduction less efficient than in metals. In liquids, energy is transferred through molecular interactions, although convection often becomes the dominant mode of heat transfer in practical systems. In gases, thermal energy is transported through molecular collisions as explained by the kinetic theory of gases. Understanding these mechanisms provides the physical foundation for thermal conductivity and helps engineers select suitable materials for heat exchangers, insulation systems, reactors, furnaces, and other heat transfer equipment.
