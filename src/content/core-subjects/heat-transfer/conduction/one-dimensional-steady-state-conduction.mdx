# One Dimensional Steady State Conduction

> **One-dimensional steady-state conduction key idea:** Under steady-state conditions, the temperature distribution through a homogeneous plane wall is **linear**, and the heat transfer rate remains **constant** throughout the wall.


For a plane wall, the heat transfer rate is given by:

$$
\boxed{q=\frac{kA\left(T_1-T_2\right)}{L}}
$$

Many engineering components such as **walls, furnace linings, pipes, and insulation** can be approximated as one-dimensional. This simplification makes heat transfer calculations much easier while providing accurate results for many practical designs.

## How much heat escapes through a house wall every hour?

Imagine a house during winter.

The inside of the house is maintained at **25°C**, while the outside temperature is only **5°C**.

Even though the wall looks solid, heat continuously escapes from the warmer interior to the colder surroundings.

As an engineer, you may ask:

- How much heat is lost every hour?
- Will increasing the wall thickness reduce heat loss?
- Should insulation be added?
- How can we calculate the heat loss accurately?

These questions are answered using one of the simplest and most important models in heat transfer:

**One-Dimensional Steady-State Conduction.**

---

# What is One-Dimensional Steady-State Conduction?

One-dimensional steady-state conduction describes heat transfer through a material when:

- Heat flows in only **one direction**.
- Temperatures do **not change with time**.
- Material properties remain constant.

This model is widely used because many engineering components closely satisfy these assumptions.

Examples include:

- Building walls
- Furnace walls
- Insulated pipelines
- Heat exchanger walls
- Reactor walls

---

# Why "One-Dimensional"?

Heat transfer is called **one-dimensional (1D)** when heat flows in only one spatial direction.

For a plane wall,

- Heat enters one face.
- Heat travels through the thickness.
- Heat leaves the opposite face.

There is **no significant heat flow along the height or width** of the wall.

# Why "Steady-State"?

A process is at **steady state** when temperatures at every location remain constant with time.

For example,

If the left surface remains at **200°C** and the right surface remains at **50°C**, the temperature inside the wall eventually reaches a stable distribution.

After that,

- Temperatures no longer change.
- Heat entering the wall equals heat leaving the wall.
- No thermal energy is stored inside the wall.

<InfoBox title="Key Idea">
At steady state, the temperature at every point inside the wall remains constant with time, even though heat continues to flow.
</InfoBox>

---

# Assumptions of One-Dimensional Steady-State Conduction

To simplify the analysis, the following assumptions are made:

- Heat flows in only one direction.
- The process is steady-state.
- Thermal conductivity remains constant.
- There is no internal heat generation.
- The wall has a uniform cross-sectional area.
- Heat transfer occurs only by conduction.

These assumptions allow us to derive a simple engineering equation.

---

# Temperature Profile in a Plane Wall

When thermal conductivity is constant and there is no heat generation,

the temperature decreases **linearly** from the hot surface to the cold surface.

This means that equal distances inside the wall experience equal temperature drops.

# Engineering Equation

Starting from Fourier's Law,

for one-dimensional steady-state conduction through a plane wall with constant thermal conductivity,

the heat transfer rate becomes:

$$
q = \frac{kA(T_1 - T_2)}{L}
$$

where,

- **q** = Heat transfer rate (W)
- **k** = Thermal conductivity (W/m·K)
- **A** = Cross-sectional area (m²)
- **L** = Wall thickness (m)
- **T₁** = Hot surface temperature (K or °C)
- **T₂** = Cold surface temperature (K or °C)



---

# Understanding the Equation

The equation reveals several important engineering relationships.

### Higher Thermal Conductivity

Higher **k**

↓

Higher heat transfer

---

### Larger Surface Area

Larger **A**

↓

More heat passes through the wall.

---

### Larger Temperature Difference

Greater difference between **T₁** and **T₂**

↓

Greater heat transfer.

---

### Greater Wall Thickness

Larger **L**

↓

Lower heat transfer.

Thicker walls provide greater resistance to heat flow.

---

# Practical Example

Consider two walls made of the same material.

Both have the same surface area and experience the same temperature difference.

However,

- Wall A is **100 mm** thick.
- Wall B is **200 mm** thick.

According to the equation,

Wall B transfers only about half as much heat as Wall A.

This is why buildings in cold regions often use thick insulated walls.

---

# Engineering Applications

One-dimensional steady-state conduction is used extensively in engineering.

Common applications include:

- Heat loss through building walls
- Furnace wall design
- Boiler insulation
- Steam pipelines
- Heat exchanger walls
- Reactor vessels
- Refrigeration systems
- Cold storage rooms

Whenever heat flows through a flat wall under nearly steady conditions, this model provides an excellent approximation.

<IndustrialInsight title="Why Engineers Use This Model">
One-dimensional steady-state conduction is the starting point for designing insulated pipelines, furnaces, reactors, storage tanks, and buildings. More complex conduction problems are often solved by extending this simple model.
</IndustrialInsight>

---

# Limitations

This model becomes inaccurate when:

- Material properties vary significantly with temperature.
- Heat generation occurs inside the wall.
- Heat flows in multiple directions.
- Temperatures change with time.

Such situations require more advanced conduction models.

---

# Key Takeaways

- One-dimensional conduction assumes heat flows in only one direction.
- Steady-state means temperatures remain constant with time.
- Constant thermal conductivity leads to a linear temperature profile.
- Heat transfer increases with thermal conductivity, area, and temperature difference.
- Heat transfer decreases as wall thickness increases.
