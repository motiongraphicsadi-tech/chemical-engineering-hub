# Introduction

Vapor-Liquid Equilibrium (VLE) describes the condition where a liquid phase and a vapor phase coexist in equilibrium.

At equilibrium:

- Evaporation occurs continuously.
- Condensation occurs continuously.
- No net mass transfer exists between phases.

VLE is one of the most important concepts in chemical engineering because most separation processes rely on vapor-liquid equilibrium behavior.

Examples include:

- Distillation
- Absorption
- Stripping
- Evaporation
- Flash separation

---

# Why It Matters

VLE calculations are essential for:

- Distillation column design
- Separation process design
- Process simulation
- Petroleum refining
- Natural gas processing

Engineers use VLE data to determine:

- Phase compositions
- Boiling temperatures
- Condensation conditions
- Separation feasibility

Without VLE principles, modern separation processes could not be designed effectively.

---

# Core Concept

When vapor and liquid coexist at equilibrium:

Temperature is equal:

$$
T_V=T_L
$$

Pressure is equal:

$$
P_V=P_L
$$

Chemical potential is equal:

$$
\mu_V=\mu_L
$$

At equilibrium:

$$
Rate\ of\ Evaporation
=
Rate\ of\ Condensation
$$

This condition defines vapor-liquid equilibrium.

---

# Working Principle

Consider a closed vessel containing a liquid.

Initially:

- Molecules escape from the liquid surface.
- Vapor concentration increases.

As vapor accumulates:

- Condensation begins.

Eventually:

$$
Evaporation = Condensation
$$

At this point:

- Vapor composition remains constant.
- Liquid composition remains constant.
- Equilibrium is established.

---

# Mathematical Definition

For component \(i\):

$$
f_i^V=f_i^L
$$

Where:

- \(f_i^V\) = Fugacity in Vapor Phase
- \(f_i^L\) = Fugacity in Liquid Phase

This equality is the fundamental criterion for VLE.

For ideal systems, simpler equations such as Raoult's Law may be used.

---

# VLE Fundamentals

VLE describes how components distribute between:

- Vapor Phase
- Liquid Phase

The distribution depends on:

- Temperature
- Pressure
- Composition

More volatile components preferentially enter the vapor phase.

Less volatile components remain in the liquid phase.

This behavior forms the basis of separation processes.

---

# Bubble Point

The Bubble Point is the condition at which the first bubble of vapor forms from a liquid mixture.

At the bubble point:

- Liquid composition is known.
- Vapor begins to form.

Mathematically:

$$
\sum y_i = 1
$$

The bubble point is important for:

- Distillation calculations
- Flash calculations
- VLE analysis

---

# Dew Point

The Dew Point is the condition at which the first drop of liquid forms from a vapor mixture.

At the dew point:

- Vapor composition is known.
- Liquid begins to condense.

Applications:

- Condenser design
- Gas processing
- Refrigeration systems

Dew point calculations are widely used in process engineering.

---

# Relative Volatility

Relative volatility measures the ease of separation between two components.

For components A and B:

$$
\alpha_{AB}
=
\frac{K_A}{K_B}
$$

Where:

$$
K=\frac{y}{x}
$$

Interpretation:

- \(\alpha > 1\) → Separation possible
- Larger \(\alpha\) → Easier separation

Relative volatility is one of the most important parameters in distillation design.

---

# Raoult's Law

Raoult's Law applies to ideal liquid solutions.

For component \(i\):

$$
P_i=x_iP_i^{sat}
$$

Where:

- \(P_i\) = Partial Pressure
- \(x_i\) = Liquid Mole Fraction
- \(P_i^{sat}\) = Saturation Pressure

Total pressure:

$$
P=\sum P_i
$$

Raoult's Law is widely used for ideal mixture calculations.

---

# Henry's Law

Henry's Law describes the solubility of dilute gases in liquids.

$$
P_i=H_ix_i
$$

Where:

- \(H_i\) = Henry's Constant

Applications:

- Gas absorption
- Environmental engineering
- Dissolved gas calculations

Henry's Law is especially useful for dilute systems.

---

# Ideal Solutions

Ideal solutions obey:

- Raoult's Law
- No excess properties
- Similar molecular interactions

Characteristics:

$$
\gamma_i=1
$$

Examples:

- Benzene-Toluene mixtures

Ideal solutions are easier to analyze mathematically.

---

# Non-Ideal Solutions

Real solutions often deviate from ideality.

Reasons:

- Molecular size differences
- Polarity differences
- Hydrogen bonding

For non-ideal systems:

$$
\gamma_i \neq 1
$$

Activity coefficient models are required.

Examples:

- Ethanol-Water
- Acetone-Water

# VLE Diagrams

VLE behavior is commonly represented using graphical diagrams.

Important diagrams include:

- T-x-y Diagram
- P-x-y Diagram
- x-y Diagram

These diagrams are extensively used in separation process design.

---

# T-x-y Diagram

The Temperature-Composition Diagram shows equilibrium behavior at constant pressure.

Variables:

- Temperature
- Liquid Composition (\(x\))
- Vapor Composition (\(y\))

Applications:

- Distillation analysis
- Bubble point calculations
- Dew point calculations

T-x-y diagrams are among the most widely used VLE diagrams.

---

# P-x-y Diagram

The Pressure-Composition Diagram shows equilibrium behavior at constant temperature.

Variables:

- Pressure
- Liquid Composition
- Vapor Composition

Applications:

- Flash calculations
- VLE analysis
- Pressure-dependent separations

---

# x-y Diagram

The x-y Diagram directly compares:

$$
x
$$

Liquid Composition

and

$$
y
$$

Vapor Composition

Applications:

- McCabe-Thiele Method
- Distillation design
- Stage calculations

This is one of the most important diagrams used in separation engineering.

# Distillation

Distillation separates components based on volatility differences.

VLE determines:

- Number of stages
- Reflux ratio
- Product purity
- Energy requirements

Distillation is the largest industrial application of VLE.

---

# Absorption

Absorption transfers a component from:

- Gas Phase
- To Liquid Phase

Applications:

- Gas purification
- CO₂ removal
- Sulfur removal

Henry's Law is commonly used in absorption calculations.

---

# Stripping

Stripping transfers a component from:

- Liquid Phase
- To Gas Phase

Applications:

- Solvent recovery
- Wastewater treatment
- VOC removal

Stripping is essentially the reverse of absorption.

# Solved Example

A binary mixture has:

$$
y_A=0.80
$$

$$
x_A=0.50
$$

Determine the equilibrium constant.

Using:

$$
K_A=\frac{y_A}{x_A}
$$

Substituting:

$$
K_A=\frac{0.80}{0.50}
$$

$$
K_A=1.60
$$

The component is more concentrated in the vapor phase.

---

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Confuse bubble point and dew point
- Mix up liquid and vapor compositions
- Misapply Raoult's Law to non-ideal systems
- Ignore activity coefficients
- Misinterpret VLE diagrams

<WarningBox>
Bubble point calculations start from liquid composition, while dew point calculations start from vapor composition.
</WarningBox>
