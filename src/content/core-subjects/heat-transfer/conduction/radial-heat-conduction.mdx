# Radial heat conduction 

>Since the **heat transfer area varies with radius** in cylinders and spheres, the plane-wall equation cannot be used. Therefore, separate conduction equations are derived for these geometries.
 
 one-dimensional steady-state radial conduction through a **hollow cylinder**,

$$
\boxed{
q =
\frac{2\pi kL\left(T_1-T_2\right)}
{\ln\left(\dfrac{r_2}{r_1}\right)}
}
$$



For one-dimensional steady-state radial conduction through a **hollow sphere**,

$$
\boxed{
q =
\frac{4\pi k\left(T_1-T_2\right)}
{\left(\dfrac{1}{r_1}-\dfrac{1}{r_2}\right)}
}
$$

## Why can't a pipeline be analyzed like a flat wall?

Suppose hot steam flows through a steel pipeline.

To reduce heat loss, the pipe is covered with insulation.

Now imagine trying to analyze this pipe using the equation for a **plane wall**.

Would it give the correct answer?

**No.**

Unlike a flat wall, the area available for heat flow changes continuously as heat moves outward through the pipe.

This changing area makes cylindrical conduction fundamentally different from plane wall conduction.

The same idea also applies to spherical equipment such as storage tanks and pressure vessels.

---

# Why Plane Wall Equations Do Not Work

For a plane wall,

- Heat flows in one direction.
- The cross-sectional area remains constant.

Therefore, the heat transfer equation is relatively simple.

However, in a cylinder or sphere,

- Heat flows **radially**.
- The heat transfer area increases with radius.

Since the area changes continuously,

the plane wall equation can no longer be used.

---

# What is Radial Conduction?

**Radial conduction** is the transfer of heat in the radial direction—from the inner surface toward the outer surface (or vice versa).

Instead of moving along a straight path,

heat spreads outward as the radius increases.

Examples include:

- Pipes
- Boilers
- Reactor vessels
- Heat exchanger tubes
- Storage tanks
- Insulated pipelines

<InfoBox title="Key Idea">
In cylindrical and spherical bodies, heat flows radially because the heat transfer area changes continuously with radius.
</InfoBox>

# Heat Conduction Through a Cylinder

Consider a hot pipe carrying steam.

Heat travels through:

- Pipe wall
- Insulation
- Surrounding air

As heat moves outward,

the radius becomes larger.

Therefore,

the heat transfer area also becomes larger.

# Engineering Equation for Cylindrical Conduction

For steady-state heat conduction through a hollow cylinder,

the heat transfer rate is

$$
q=\frac{2\pi kL(T_1-T_2)}
{\ln\left(\dfrac{r_2}{r_1}\right)}
$$

where,

- **q** = Heat transfer rate (W)
- **k** = Thermal conductivity (W/m·K)
- **L** = Length of the cylinder (m)
- **r₁** = Inner radius (m)
- **r₂** = Outer radius (m)
- **T₁** = Inner surface temperature
- **T₂** = Outer surface temperature

Notice that the radius appears inside a logarithmic term because the heat transfer area changes continuously.

# Heat Conduction Through a Sphere

Spherical conduction occurs whenever heat flows through spherical objects.

Examples include:

- Cryogenic storage tanks
- LNG storage spheres
- Hot water storage tanks
- Spherical pressure vessels

As in cylinders,

the heat transfer area increases with radius.

# Engineering Equation for Spherical Conduction

For steady-state conduction through a hollow sphere,

$$
q=
\frac{4\pi k(T_1-T_2)}
{\left(\dfrac{1}{r_1}-\dfrac{1}{r_2}\right)}
$$

where,

- **r₁** = Inner radius
- **r₂** = Outer radius

The changing surface area makes this equation different from both plane walls and cylinders.



---

# Effect of Radius on Heat Transfer

One of the biggest differences between plane walls and cylinders is the effect of radius.

For a cylinder,

Surface area is

$$
A=2\pi rL
$$

As the radius increases,

the heat transfer area also increases.

Similarly, for a sphere,

Surface area is

$$
A=4\pi r^2
$$

Therefore,

larger radii provide larger areas for heat flow.

This continuously changing area is the reason different equations are required.

---

# What is the Log Mean Area Concept?

Since the heat transfer area changes continuously through a cylindrical wall,

there is no single constant area that accurately represents the entire wall.

Instead,

engineers often use the **logarithmic mean area (log mean area)** to simplify calculations.

It provides an equivalent average area for radial heat conduction.

Although the complete derivation is beyond the scope of this chapter,

the concept explains why logarithmic terms naturally appear in cylindrical conduction equations.

<InfoBox title="Remember">
The logarithmic term in the cylindrical conduction equation arises because the heat transfer area increases continuously with radius.
</InfoBox>

---

# Plane Wall vs Cylinder

| Plane Wall | Cylinder |
|------------|----------|
| Constant area | Area increases with radius |
| Straight heat flow | Radial heat flow |
| Simple thickness | Inner and outer radius |
| Linear geometry | Curved geometry |
| No logarithmic term | Logarithmic term appears |

# Industrial Applications

Radial conduction is encountered in almost every chemical processing plant.

Common examples include:

- Steam pipelines
- Reactor vessels
- Boiler tubes
- Heat exchanger tubes
- Distillation columns
- Cryogenic storage tanks
- LPG and LNG storage spheres
- Pressure vessels

Most industrial thermal equipment has cylindrical geometry rather than flat walls.

<IndustrialInsight title="Why Cylindrical Conduction Matters">
Most equipment in chemical plants—such as pipes, reactors, heat exchangers, pressure vessels, boilers, and storage tanks—is cylindrical. Accurate thermal design therefore requires radial conduction analysis instead of plane wall equations.
</IndustrialInsight>

# Key Takeaways

- Plane wall equations cannot be applied directly to pipes and spheres.
- Cylindrical and spherical bodies experience radial heat conduction.
- Heat transfer area increases with radius.
- Cylindrical conduction introduces a logarithmic term.
- Most industrial equipment requires cylindrical conduction analysis.
