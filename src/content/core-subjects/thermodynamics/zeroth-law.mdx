# Introduction

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics is one of the fundamental laws of thermodynamics.

Although discovered after the First and Second Laws, it was placed before them because it provides the basis for the concept of temperature.

The Zeroth Law explains:

- Thermal equilibrium
- Temperature comparison
- Temperature measurement
- Operation of thermometers

Without the Zeroth Law, temperature would not be a meaningful thermodynamic property.

---

# Why It Matters

Temperature is one of the most frequently measured variables in engineering.

Industrial processes rely heavily on temperature measurements for:

- Process control
- Reactor operation
- Distillation
- Heat exchangers
- Boilers
- Refrigeration systems

The Zeroth Law provides the scientific basis for all temperature measurement techniques.

---

# Core Concept

The Zeroth Law introduces the concept of thermal equilibrium.

If two systems are separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are also in thermal equilibrium with each other.

This principle allows temperature comparison between systems.

It forms the foundation of temperature measurement.

---

# Working Principle

Consider three systems:

- System A
- System B
- System C

If:

$$
T_A = T_C
$$

and

$$
T_B = T_C
$$

then:

$$
T_A = T_B
$$

This means all three systems have the same temperature and are in thermal equilibrium.

The thermometer acts as the third system during temperature measurement.

---

# Mathematical Definition

The Zeroth Law can be expressed mathematically as:

$$
T_A = T_C
$$

$$
T_B = T_C
$$

Therefore:

$$
T_A = T_B
$$

This simple relation provides the basis for defining temperature as a measurable property.

---

# Statement of Zeroth Law

The formal statement is:

"If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, then they are in thermal equilibrium with each other."

This statement allows engineers to compare temperatures without placing systems directly in contact.

---

# Thermal Equilibrium

Thermal equilibrium exists when no net heat transfer occurs between systems.

Condition:

$$
T_1 = T_2
$$

At thermal equilibrium:

$$
Q = 0
$$

where:

- \(Q\) = Heat Transfer Rate

No temperature difference means no driving force for heat transfer.

---

# Concept of Temperature

Temperature is a measure of the thermal condition of a system.

It indicates:

- Hotness
- Coldness
- Direction of heat flow

Heat always flows from:

Higher Temperature → Lower Temperature

until thermal equilibrium is achieved.

Temperature is an intensive property.

---

# Temperature Measurement

Temperature cannot be measured directly.

Instead, engineers measure properties that change with temperature.

Examples:

- Expansion of liquids
- Electrical resistance
- Thermoelectric voltage
- Infrared radiation

These measurable changes are correlated with temperature.

---

# Thermometers

A thermometer is a device used to measure temperature.

It operates by reaching thermal equilibrium with the object being measured.

Common thermometers include:

- Mercury Thermometer
- Alcohol Thermometer
- Resistance Thermometer (RTD)
- Thermocouple
- Infrared Thermometer

Thermometers rely on the Zeroth Law for accurate operation.

---

# Temperature Scales

Several temperature scales are used in engineering and science.

Common scales include:

- Celsius
- Fahrenheit
- Kelvin
- Rankine

Each scale has a different reference point and application.

---

# Celsius Scale

The Celsius scale is widely used in scientific and engineering applications.

Reference points:

- Ice Point = 0°C
- Boiling Point of Water = 100°C

at standard atmospheric pressure.

The interval between these points is divided into 100 equal divisions.

---

# Fahrenheit Scale

The Fahrenheit scale is commonly used in some countries.

Reference points:

- Ice Point = 32°F
- Boiling Point = 212°F

The interval is divided into 180 equal divisions.

---

# Kelvin Scale

The Kelvin scale is the absolute thermodynamic temperature scale.

Reference point:

$$
0K
$$

corresponds to absolute zero.

Relationship:

$$
K = °C + 273.15
$$

Kelvin is the preferred temperature scale in thermodynamic calculations.

---

# Rankine Scale

The Rankine scale is the absolute temperature scale corresponding to Fahrenheit.

Relationship:

$$
R = °F + 459.67
$$

It is primarily used in some engineering applications.

---

# Temperature Conversion Relations

Common temperature conversion equations include:

Celsius to Fahrenheit:

$$
°F = \frac{9}{5}(°C) + 32
$$

Fahrenheit to Celsius:

$$
°C = \frac{5}{9}(°F - 32)
$$

Celsius to Kelvin:

$$
K = °C + 273.15
$$

Kelvin to Celsius:

$$
°C = K - 273.15
$$

Fahrenheit to Rankine:

$$
R = °F + 459.67
$$

Rankine to Fahrenheit:

$$
°F = R - 459.67
$$

These conversions are frequently used in engineering calculations.

# Advantages

The Zeroth Law provides:

- Basis for temperature measurement

- Standardized temperature scales

---

# Limitations

The Zeroth Law:

- Does not quantify heat transfer
- Does not predict energy changes
- Does not determine process efficiency

These topics are covered by the First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics.

# Engineering Perspective

Temperature affects:

- Reaction rates

Accurate temperature measurement is essential for plant operation and process control.

The Zeroth Law forms the foundation of all temperature instrumentation.

# Solved Example

Convert:

$$
25^\circ C
$$

to Kelvin.

Using:

$$
K = °C + 273.15
$$

Substituting:

$$
K = 25 + 273.15
$$

$$
K = 298.15
$$

Therefore:

$$
25^\circ C = 298.15K
$$

---

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Confuse heat and temperature
- Forget absolute temperature scales
- Use incorrect conversion equations
- Assume thermal equilibrium occurs instantly
- Use Celsius instead of Kelvin in thermodynamic calculations

<WarningBox>
Kelvin must be used in most thermodynamic equations because it is an absolute temperature scale.
</WarningBox>
