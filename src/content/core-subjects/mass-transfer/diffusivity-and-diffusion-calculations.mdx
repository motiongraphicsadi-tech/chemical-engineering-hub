# Diffusivity and Diffusion Calculations Introduction

The rate at which molecules diffuse depends on a property known as diffusivity or diffusion coefficient.

Just as viscosity measures resistance to momentum transfer and thermal conductivity measures heat transfer capability, diffusivity measures the ability of a species to diffuse through a medium.

Diffusivity is one of the most important parameters in mass transfer calculations because it directly influences diffusion rates.

Engineers frequently use diffusivity data to design:

- Distillation columns
- Absorption towers
- Extractors
- Membrane systems
- Dryers
- Catalytic reactors

Accurate estimation of diffusivity is essential for reliable process design.

---

# Why It Matters

Diffusivity determines how rapidly species move within gases, liquids, and solids.

It affects:

- Separation efficiency
- Equipment size
- Process economics
- Reactor performance
- Product quality

Industrial importance:

- Higher diffusivity improves mass transfer rates.
- Lower diffusivity may require larger equipment.
- Accurate diffusivity values improve design accuracy.

Diffusivity calculations are heavily used in:

- GATE Chemical Engineering
- Transport Phenomena
- Mass Transfer Operations
- Industrial process design

# Core Concept

Diffusivity measures the ease with which one species moves through another.

It is represented as:

$$
D_{AB}
$$

where:

- A = Diffusing species
- B = Medium through which diffusion occurs

Higher diffusivity means:

- Faster molecular movement
- Faster mass transfer
- Lower diffusion resistance

Lower diffusivity means:

- Slower molecular transport
- Higher mass transfer resistance

Diffusivity is analogous to thermal conductivity in heat transfer.

# Classification and Types

## Gas Diffusivity

Diffusion between gaseous species.

Examples:

- Oxygen in air
- Ammonia in air
- Hydrogen in nitrogen

Typical values:

$$
10^{-5}
\;m^2/s
$$

---

## Liquid Diffusivity

Diffusion within liquids.

Examples:

- Salt in water
- Ethanol in water

Typical values:

$$
10^{-9}
\text{ to }
10^{-10}
\;m^2/s
$$

---

## Solid Diffusivity

Diffusion through solids.

Examples:

- Carbon diffusion in steel
- Hydrogen diffusion in metals

Typical values:

$$
10^{-12}
\text{ to }
10^{-20}
\;m^2/s
$$

---

## Molecular Diffusivity

Diffusion caused by molecular motion.

---

## Effective Diffusivity

Used in porous media.

$$
D_e
=
\frac{\varepsilon}{\tau}
D_{AB}
$$

Where:

- \(\varepsilon\) = Porosity
- \(\tau\) = Tortuosity

---

# Working Principle

Diffusion calculations follow these steps:

1. Identify the diffusing species.
2. Determine operating conditions.
3. Obtain diffusivity data.
4. Calculate concentration gradients.
5. Apply Fick's Law.
6. Determine diffusion flux or rate.

The diffusion coefficient controls the magnitude of transfer.

---

# Mathematical Definition

Diffusivity appears in Fick's First Law:

$$
N_A
=
-D_{AB}
\frac{dC_A}{dz}
$$

Where:

| Symbol | Description | Unit |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| $N_A$ | Diffusion Flux | mol/m²·s |
| $D_{AB}$ | Diffusivity | m²/s |
| $C_A$ | Concentration | mol/m³ |
| $z$ | Distance | m |

The SI unit of diffusivity is:

$$
m^2/s
$$

# Assumptions

Common assumptions include:

- Isothermal conditions
- Constant diffusivity

- Homogeneous medium
- One-dimensional diffusion
- Steady-state transfer

These assumptions simplify diffusion calculations.

---

# Derivation and Theory

Experimental observations show:

$$
N_A
\propto
\frac{dC_A}{dz}
$$

Introducing diffusivity:

$$
N_A
=
-D_{AB}
\frac{dC_A}{dz}
$$

Thus diffusivity becomes the proportionality constant relating concentration gradient and diffusion flux.

Large diffusivity values produce greater mass transfer rates.

## Gas Diffusion Dependence

Approximately:

$$
D_{AB}
\propto
\frac{T^{3/2}}{P}
$$

This relationship explains temperature and pressure effects.

# Graphical Interpretation

### Diffusivity vs Temperature

As temperature increases:

$$
D_{AB}
\uparrow
$$

Diffusivity increases.

---

### Diffusivity vs Pressure

For gases:

$$
P \uparrow
\Rightarrow
D_{AB}\downarrow
$$

Higher pressure reduces diffusivity.

---

### Diffusion Flux vs Concentration Gradient

Steeper concentration gradients produce larger fluxes.

The relationship is approximately linear.

# Solved Example

### Problem

Oxygen diffuses through air.

Given:

$$
D_{AB}
=
2.0\times10^{-5}
\;m^2/s
$$

Film thickness:

$$
L
=
0.01\,m
$$

Concentration difference:

$$
C_{A1}-C_{A2}
=
50
\;mol/m^3
$$

Calculate diffusion flux.

### Given

- \(D_{AB}=2.0\times10^{-5}\;m^2/s\)
- \(L=0.01\,m\)
- \(\Delta C=50\,mol/m^3\)

### Solution

Using:

$$
N_A
=
D_{AB}
\frac{\Delta C}{L}
$$

Substituting:

$$
N_A
=
\frac{(2\times10^{-5})(50)}
{0.01}
$$

$$
N_A
=
0.10
\;mol/m^2.s
$$

### Final Answer

$$
N_A
=
0.10
\;mol/m^2.s
$$
