# Introduction

Energy balances are among the most important tools used by chemical engineers.

Every industrial process involves energy entering, leaving, or accumulating within a system.

Energy balance calculations help engineers determine:

- Heating requirements
- Cooling requirements
- Power consumption
- Equipment performance
- Process efficiency

Energy balances are based directly on the First Law of Thermodynamics.

---

# Why It Matters

Energy costs represent a major portion of industrial operating expenses.

Engineers use energy balances to:

- Reduce utility consumption
- Improve efficiency
- Size equipment
- Troubleshoot processes
- Optimize plant operations

Energy balance calculations are performed in:

- Refineries
- Power plants
- Fertilizer plants
- Petrochemical industries
- Pharmaceutical industries

---

# Core Concept

The fundamental principle behind energy balances is:

$$
Energy\ In
-
Energy\ Out
=
Accumulation
$$

This equation applies to all thermodynamic systems.

The energy entering a system must either:

- Leave the system
- Accumulate within the system

Energy cannot disappear.

---

# Working Principle

Energy balance calculations generally follow these steps:

1. Define the system boundary.
2. Identify all energy inputs.
3. Identify all energy outputs.
4. Determine accumulation.
5. Apply the First Law.
6. Solve for the unknown quantity.

This structured approach is used in industrial process analysis.

---

# Mathematical Definition

The general energy balance equation is:

$$
Input
-
Output
=
Accumulation
$$

More formally:

$$
Energy\ In
-
Energy\ Out
=
\frac{dE_{system}}{dt}
$$

This equation is the foundation of all energy balance calculations.

---

# General Energy Balance Equation

For any thermodynamic system:

$$
Q - W + E_{in} - E_{out}
=
Accumulation
$$

Where:

- $Q$ = Heat Transfer
- $W$ = Work Transfer
- $E_{in}$ = Energy Entering with Mass
- $E_{out}$ = Energy Leaving with Mass

This equation applies to both open and closed systems.

---

# Accumulation Term

Accumulation represents energy stored within the system.

Mathematically:

$$
Accumulation
=
\frac{dE}{dt}
$$

If system energy increases:

$$
Accumulation > 0
$$

If system energy decreases:

$$
Accumulation < 0
$$

Accumulation becomes zero at steady state.

---

# Input and Output Terms

Energy may enter or leave a system through:

- Heat Transfer
- Work Transfer
- Mass Flow

Energy associated with mass flow includes:

$$
h
$$

Enthalpy

$$
\frac{V^2}{2}
$$

Kinetic Energy

$$
gz
$$

Potential Energy

These terms are included in open-system analyses.

---

# Steady State Energy Balance

At steady state:

$$
\frac{dE}{dt}=0
$$

Therefore:

$$
Energy\ In
=
Energy\ Out
$$

System properties do not change with time.

Most continuous industrial processes operate under steady-state conditions.

Examples:

- Pumps
- Compressors
- Turbines
- Heat Exchangers

---

# Unsteady State Energy Balance

In unsteady-state systems:

$$
\frac{dE}{dt}\neq0
$$

Energy accumulates or depletes with time.

Examples:

- Batch reactors
- Storage tanks
- Startup operations
- Shutdown operations

System properties vary continuously.

---

# Mechanical Energy Balance

Mechanical energy balances focus on:

- Pressure Energy
- Kinetic Energy
- Potential Energy

A simplified mechanical energy equation is:

$$
\frac{P}{\rho}
+
\frac{V^2}{2}
+
gz
+
W_s
=
Constant
$$

Mechanical energy balances are widely used in fluid flow systems.

---

# Shaft Work

Shaft work is mechanical work transferred through rotating equipment.

Examples:

- Pumps
- Compressors
- Turbines
- Agitators

Symbol:

$$
W_s
$$

Applications:

- Power calculations
- Equipment design
- Efficiency analysis

---

# Flow Work

Flow work is the work required to push fluid into or out of a control volume.

Mathematically:

$$
Flow\ Work = PV
$$

Per unit mass:

$$
Pv
$$

Flow work is included within enthalpy.

Therefore:

$$
h=u+Pv
$$

---

# Kinetic Energy Changes

Kinetic energy arises from fluid motion.

Per unit mass:

$$
KE=\frac{V^2}{2}
$$

Where:

- \(V\) = Velocity

Kinetic energy changes are important in:

- Nozzles
- Diffusers
- Jet systems

---

# Potential Energy Changes

Potential energy arises from elevation.

Per unit mass:

$$
PE=gz
$$

Where:

- \(g\) = Gravitational acceleration
- \(z\) = Elevation

Potential energy changes are important in:

- Pumping systems
- Hydroelectric systems
- Elevated storage tanks

---

# Process Applications

Energy balances are used throughout process industries.

Important applications include:

- Mixing
- Heating
- Cooling
- Expansion
- Compression

---

# Mixing

Mixing processes combine streams having different:

- Temperatures
- Flow rates
- Compositions

Energy balances determine:

- Outlet temperature
- Heating requirements
- Cooling requirements

Mixing calculations are common in chemical plants.

---

# Heating

Heating operations increase fluid temperature.

Examples:

- Boilers
- Heaters
- Furnaces

Energy balances determine:

$$
Q_{required}
$$

for the process.

---

# Cooling

Cooling removes thermal energy from a system.

Examples:

- Coolers
- Condensers
- Refrigeration systems

Energy balances determine:

$$
Q_{removed}
$$

from the process stream.

---

# Expansion

Expansion processes occur in:

- Turbines
- Nozzles

Pressure energy decreases while:

- Velocity may increase
- Work may be produced

Energy balances predict outlet conditions.

---

# Compression

Compression processes occur in:

- Compressors
- Pumps

Mechanical work is supplied to increase pressure.

Energy balances determine:

$$
W_{required}
$$

for operation.

---

Energy balances are applied in:

- Boilers

- Pumps
- Heat Exchangers

Virtually every industrial process requires energy balance analysis.

# Advantages

Energy balances help engineers:

- Predict energy requirements
- Improve efficiency
- Reduce utility costs
- Design equipment
- Optimize processes

They form the basis of process engineering calculations.

---

# Limitations

Energy balances:

- Do not indicate process direction
- Do not predict process feasibility
- Ignore entropy generation
- Cannot determine maximum efficiency

These topics require the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

---

# Engineering Perspective

Chemical engineers routinely perform energy balances during:

- Process design
- Process simulation
- Utility optimization
- Equipment sizing
- Troubleshooting

Energy balances are among the most frequently used engineering calculations in industry.

# Solved Example

A heater supplies:

$$
Q=5000\;kJ/hr
$$

to a process stream.

No shaft work is involved.

Assuming steady state:

$$
Accumulation = 0
$$

Therefore:

$$
Energy\ In = Energy\ Out
$$

The process stream gains:

$$
5000\;kJ/hr
$$

of thermal energy.

---

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Ignore accumulation terms
- Use incorrect sign conventions
- Forget kinetic energy terms
- Forget potential energy terms
- Misapply steady-state assumptions

<WarningBox>
Never assume steady-state conditions unless the problem explicitly indicates that system properties remain constant with time.
</WarningBox>

# Key Takeaways

- Energy balances are based on the First Law of Thermodynamics.
- Energy In − Energy Out = Accumulation.
- Steady-state systems have zero accumulation.
- Unsteady-state systems have changing energy content.
- Shaft work is important in rotating equipment.
- Flow work is included within enthalpy.
- Kinetic and potential energy changes may be significant in some systems.
- Energy balances are essential for industrial process analysis.

Energy balances are fundamental engineering tools based on the First Law of Thermodynamics.
