# Introduction

Phase Equilibrium is one of the most important topics in thermodynamics and chemical engineering.

Many industrial processes involve multiple phases such as:

- Liquid-Vapor
- Liquid-Liquid
- Solid-Liquid
- Solid-Vapor

Phase equilibrium describes the condition under which two or more phases coexist without any net change in their properties over time.

Understanding phase equilibrium is essential for:

- Distillation
- Crystallization
- Evaporation
- Condensation
- Extraction

---

# Why It Matters

Chemical engineers frequently deal with systems containing multiple phases.

Examples include:

- Boiling water
- Distillation columns
- Refrigeration systems
- Separation processes
- Petroleum refining

Phase equilibrium helps engineers determine:

- Phase compositions
- Boiling points
- Condensation conditions
- Separation feasibility

It forms the foundation of phase equilibrium calculations used throughout the process industries.

---

# Core Concept

When two or more phases coexist and there is no net transfer of matter between them, the system is said to be in phase equilibrium.

At equilibrium:

- Temperature remains constant
- Pressure remains constant
- Chemical potential remains equal across phases

For equilibrium:

$$
T^\alpha = T^\beta
$$

$$
P^\alpha = P^\beta
$$

$$
\mu^\alpha = \mu^\beta
$$

Where:

- \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) represent different phases

---

# Working Principle

Phase equilibrium occurs when the driving force for phase change becomes zero.

For example:

Liquid Water ⇌ Water Vapor

Initially:

- Evaporation occurs
- Condensation occurs

At equilibrium:

$$
Rate\ of\ Evaporation
=
Rate\ of\ Condensation
$$

The amount of each phase remains constant.

---

# Mathematical Definition

The condition for phase equilibrium is:

$$
\mu_i^\alpha
=
\mu_i^\beta
$$

Where:

- \(\mu_i\) = Chemical Potential of Component \(i\)

This equality must hold for every component in every phase.

This is the fundamental mathematical criterion of phase equilibrium.

---

# Definition of Phase Equilibrium

Phase equilibrium is the state in which multiple phases coexist without any net transfer of matter between them.

Characteristics:

- Constant temperature
- Constant pressure
- Equal chemical potentials
- No observable macroscopic changes

Examples:

- Ice and water at 0°C
- Water and steam at boiling point
- Saturated liquid and vapor mixtures

---

# Phase Rule

The Phase Rule provides the relationship between:

- Components
- Phases
- Degrees of Freedom

It helps determine how many variables can be changed independently while maintaining equilibrium.

The Phase Rule is one of the most important equations in phase equilibrium.

---

# Gibbs Phase Rule

The Gibbs Phase Rule is:

$$
F=C-P+2
$$

Where:

- \(F\) = Degrees of Freedom
- \(C\) = Number of Components
- \(P\) = Number of Phases

This equation applies to non-reacting systems.

It is widely used in phase equilibrium analysis.

---

# Degrees of Freedom

Degrees of Freedom represent the number of independent variables that can be changed without altering the number of phases present.

Examples of variables:

- Temperature
- Pressure
- Composition

If:

$$
F=0
$$

the system is invariant.

If:

$$
F=1
$$

the system is univariant.

If:

$$
F=2
$$

the system is bivariant.

---

# Single Component Systems

A single-component system contains only one chemical species.

Example:

- Pure Water
- Pure Ammonia
- Pure Carbon Dioxide

For water:

- Ice
- Liquid Water
- Water Vapor

are different phases of the same component.

For a single-component system:

$$
C=1
$$

---

# Multi-Component Systems

Multi-component systems contain more than one chemical species.

Examples:

- Ethanol-Water
- Benzene-Toluene
- Hydrocarbon Mixtures

For binary systems:

$$
C=2
$$

Multi-component systems exhibit more complex phase behavior than pure substances.

---

# Phase Diagrams

Phase diagrams graphically represent equilibrium relationships between phases.

They help engineers visualize:

- Phase boundaries
- Equilibrium regions
- Critical points
- Triple points

Common phase diagrams include:

- P-T Diagram
- P-V Diagram
- T-x-y Diagram

---

# P-T Diagram

A Pressure-Temperature diagram shows phase boundaries between:

- Solid
- Liquid
- Vapor

Important features:

- Triple Point
- Critical Point
- Sublimation Curve
- Vaporization Curve
- Fusion Curve

P-T diagrams are commonly used for pure substances.

---

# P-V Diagram

A Pressure-Volume diagram shows how pressure varies with volume.

Applications:

- Phase transition analysis
- Critical behavior studies
- Thermodynamic cycle analysis

The two-phase region is clearly visible on P-V diagrams.

These diagrams are frequently used in thermodynamics and process design.

---

# T-x-y Diagram

The Temperature-Composition diagram is used for binary mixtures.

Variables:

- Temperature
- Liquid Composition (\(x\))
- Vapor Composition (\(y\))

Applications:

- Distillation
- VLE Calculations
- Separation Design

T-x-y diagrams are fundamental tools in separation process engineering.

---

# Critical Phenomena

Critical phenomena occur near the critical point.

At the critical point:

$$
T=T_c
$$

$$
P=P_c
$$

Liquid and vapor phases become indistinguishable.

Characteristics:

- Density difference approaches zero
- Surface tension approaches zero
- Large property fluctuations occur

Critical phenomena are important in supercritical fluid applications.

# Solved Example

Determine the degrees of freedom for a pure substance existing in liquid-vapor equilibrium.

Given:

$$
C=1
$$

$$
P=2
$$

Using Gibbs Phase Rule:

$$
F=C-P+2
$$

Substituting:

$$
F=1-2+2
$$

$$
F=1
$$

Therefore:

$$
F=1
$$

The system is univariant.

Only one variable can be changed independently.

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Confuse phases with components
- Apply Gibbs Phase Rule incorrectly
- Forget the definition of degrees of freedom
- Misinterpret phase diagrams
- Confuse critical point and triple point

<WarningBox>
A component is a chemical species, whereas a phase is a physically distinct region of matter. These terms are not interchangeable.
</WarningBox>
