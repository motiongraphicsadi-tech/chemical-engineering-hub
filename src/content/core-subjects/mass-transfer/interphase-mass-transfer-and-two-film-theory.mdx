# Interphase Mass Transfer and Two-Film Theory Introduction

Many industrial mass transfer operations involve the transfer of a species from one phase to another.

Examples include:

- Absorption of ammonia into water
- Oxygen transfer into fermentation broths
- Distillation operations
- Stripping processes
- Humidification systems

In these operations, the species must cross a phase boundary known as the **interface**.

The study of how molecules move across this interface is called **interphase mass transfer**.

The most widely accepted model for describing this process is the **Two-Film Theory**, developed by Whitman.

This theory forms the foundation of modern mass transfer design and is extensively used in absorption, distillation, extraction, and stripping calculations.

---

Interphase mass transfer governs the performance of:

- Absorption towers

Almost every separation process in chemical engineering involves interphase mass transfer.

# Core Concept

Interphase mass transfer occurs when a species moves from one phase into another.

For example:

Gas Phase:

$$
A_{gas}
\rightarrow
A_{liquid}
$$

Examples:

- CO₂ absorption into water
- NH₃ absorption into acid solution
- Oxygen transfer into biological systems

The transfer occurs because a concentration difference exists between the phases.

The species must:

1. Diffuse through the gas phase.
2. Cross the interface.
3. Diffuse through the liquid phase.

Each step contributes resistance to mass transfer.

# Working Principle

According to Two-Film Theory:

1. Bulk gas is well mixed.
2. A thin stagnant gas film exists near the interface.
3. A thin stagnant liquid film exists near the interface.
4. Diffusion occurs through both films.
5. Interface is at equilibrium.
6. Species moves from high concentration to low concentration.

The transfer sequence is:

Bulk Gas

↓

Gas Film

↓

Interface

↓

Liquid Film

↓

Bulk Liquid

Mass transfer resistance exists mainly within the two films.

---

# Mathematical Definition

General flux expression:

$$
N_A
=
k_G(P_{A,b}-P_{A,i})
$$

Gas-side basis.

Where:

- \(P_{A,b}\) = Bulk gas partial pressure
- \(P_{A,i}\) = Interfacial partial pressure

---

Liquid-side basis:

$$
N_A
=
k_L(C_{A,i}-C_{A,b})
$$

Where:

- \(C_{A,i}\) = Interfacial concentration
- \(C_{A,b}\) = Bulk liquid concentration

---

Overall gas-side equation:

$$
N_A
=
K_G(P_{A,b}-P_A^*)
$$

---

Overall liquid-side equation:

$$
N_A
=
K_L(C_A^*-C_{A,b})
$$

---

# Assumptions

Two-Film Theory assumes:

- Thin stagnant films exist
- Bulk phases are perfectly mixed
- Steady-state diffusion
- Interface is at equilibrium
- Constant diffusivity
- No chemical reaction
- One-dimensional diffusion

These assumptions simplify analysis significantly.

---

# Derivation and Theory

# Gas-Liquid Interface

The interface is the boundary separating gas and liquid phases.

At the interface:

$$
\text{Gas Phase}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Liquid Phase}
$$

Molecules cross almost instantaneously.

Therefore:

### Interface Equilibrium Exists

This is a key assumption.

---

# Two-Film Theory

Whitman proposed that resistance occurs within two stagnant films:

### Gas Film Resistance

Resistance in gas phase.

### Liquid Film Resistance

Resistance in liquid phase.

The interface itself offers negligible resistance.

---

# Resistance Concept

Mass transfer resistance behaves similarly to electrical resistance.

Total resistance:

$$
R_T
=
R_G
+
R_L
$$

Where:

- \(R_G\) = Gas-side resistance
- \(R_L\) = Liquid-side resistance

---

# Rate-Controlling Resistance

The largest resistance controls the overall transfer rate.

Examples:

### Gas-Controlled System

$$
R_G \gg R_L
$$

Gas film controls.

Example:

- NH₃ absorption

---

### Liquid-Controlled System

$$
R_L \gg R_G
$$

Liquid film controls.

Example:

- Oxygen absorption in water

---

### Mixed Control

Both resistances are important.

---

# Key Relationships and Equations

## Gas-Side Flux

$$
N_A
=
k_G(P_{A,b}-P_{A,i})
$$

---

## Liquid-Side Flux

$$
N_A
=
k_L(C_{A,i}-C_{A,b})
$$

---

## Overall Gas Coefficient

$$
N_A
=
K_G(P_{A,b}-P_A^*)
$$

---

## Overall Liquid Coefficient

$$
N_A
=
K_L(C_A^*-C_{A,b})
$$

---

## Overall Gas Resistance

$$
\frac{1}{K_G}
=
\frac{1}{k_G}
+
\frac{m}{k_L}
$$

---

## Overall Liquid Resistance

$$
\frac{1}{K_L}
=
\frac{1}{k_L}
+
\frac{1}{mk_G}
$$

Where:

- \(m\) = Equilibrium line slope

# Solved Example

### Problem

For a gas absorption process:

$$
k_G=0.03
\;mol/(m^2.s.atm)
$$

Bulk gas partial pressure:

$$
P_{A,b}=0.60
\;atm
$$

Interfacial pressure:

$$
P_{A,i}=0.20
\;atm
$$

Calculate mass transfer flux.

### Given

- \(k_G=0.03\)
- \(P_{A,b}=0.60\;atm\)
- \(P_{A,i}=0.20\;atm\)

### Solution

Using:

$$
N_A
=
k_G(P_{A,b}-P_{A,i})
$$

Substituting:

$$
N_A
=
0.03(0.60-0.20)
$$

$$
N_A
=
0.012
\;mol/m^2.s
$$

### Final Answer

$$
N_A
=
0.012
\;mol/m^2.s
$$

# Common Mistakes

- Confusing interface concentration with bulk concentration
- Ignoring equilibrium relationships
- Mixing gas-side and liquid-side driving forces
- Assuming interface resistance exists
- Misidentifying controlling resistance

# Key Takeaways

- Interphase mass transfer occurs across phase boundaries.
- Two-Film Theory assumes stagnant gas and liquid films.
- The interface is assumed to be at equilibrium.
- Resistance exists primarily in the films.
- The largest resistance controls transfer rate.
- Overall coefficients combine individual resistances.
- Two-Film Theory forms the basis of industrial mass transfer design.
- Absorption and distillation calculations rely heavily on this theory.

# Summary

Interphase mass transfer involves the movement of species across phase boundaries such as gas-liquid interfaces. The Two-Film Theory explains this process by assuming thin stagnant films exist on both sides of the interface, where diffusion provides the primary resistance to transfer.

The concepts of interface equilibrium, individual resistances, overall driving force, and rate-controlling resistance form the foundation of modern mass transfer calculations and are extensively used in the design of absorbers, distillation columns, strippers, and other separation equipment.
