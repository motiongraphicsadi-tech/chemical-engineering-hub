# Introduction

Most industrial fluids exist as mixtures rather than pure substances.

Examples include:

- Ethanol-Water
- Benzene-Toluene
- Air
- Natural Gas
- Electrolyte Solutions

The thermodynamics of solutions studies how thermodynamic properties change when substances are mixed.

This subject provides the foundation for:

- Separation processes
- Phase equilibrium
- Reaction equilibrium
- Process simulation

Understanding solution behavior is essential for chemical engineering design.

---

# Why It Matters

Chemical engineers frequently work with:

- Liquid mixtures
- Gas mixtures
- Electrolyte systems
- Multicomponent process streams

Solution thermodynamics helps engineers:

- Predict phase behavior
- Design distillation columns
- Calculate equilibrium compositions
- Model chemical reactions
- Simulate industrial processes

Without solution thermodynamics, modern separation processes would not be possible.

---

# Core Concept

When substances are mixed, their properties do not always combine linearly.

Mixing causes changes in:

- Volume
- Enthalpy
- Entropy
- Gibbs Energy

The thermodynamic behavior of a solution depends on:

- Composition
- Temperature
- Pressure
- Molecular interactions

Solution thermodynamics quantifies these effects.

---

# Working Principle

A solution is formed when two or more substances are mixed.

The resulting properties depend on:

- Individual component properties
- Molecular interactions
- Composition

For ideal solutions:

$$
Property_{mixture}
=
\sum x_i Property_i
$$

For real solutions:

Additional interaction effects must be considered.

These deviations are described using activity coefficients and excess properties.

---

# Mathematical Definition

For a mixture:

$$
M
=
\sum n_i \bar{M}_i
$$

Where:

- \(M\) = Total Extensive Property
- \(n_i\) = Moles of Component \(i\)
- \(\bar{M}_i\) = Partial Molar Property

This equation forms the basis of solution thermodynamics.

---

# Partial Molar Properties

Partial molar properties describe the contribution of one component to an overall mixture property.

General definition:

$$
\bar{M}_i
=
\left(
\frac{\partial M}
{\partial n_i}
\right)_{T,P,n_j}
$$

Examples include:

- Partial Molar Volume
- Partial Molar Enthalpy
- Partial Molar Gibbs Energy

These properties are fundamental in mixture calculations.

---

# Partial Molar Volume

Partial molar volume describes the volume contribution of a component within a mixture.

Mathematically:

$$
\bar{V}_i
=
\left(
\frac{\partial V}
{\partial n_i}
\right)
_{T,P,n_j}
$$

Applications:

- Liquid mixture design
- Density calculations
- Volume change predictions

---

# Partial Molar Enthalpy

Partial molar enthalpy represents the contribution of a component to mixture enthalpy.

$$
\bar{H}_i
=
\left(
\frac{\partial H}
{\partial n_i}
\right)
_{T,P,n_j}
$$

Applications:

- Mixing calculations
- Heat balance calculations
- Process simulation

---

# Partial Molar Gibbs Energy

Partial molar Gibbs energy is one of the most important partial molar properties.

$$
\bar{G}_i
=
\left(
\frac{\partial G}
{\partial n_i}
\right)
_{T,P,n_j}
$$

This property is also known as:

$$
\mu_i
$$

the chemical potential.

# Solution Behavior

Solutions may behave either:

- Ideally
- Non-Ideally

The behavior depends primarily on molecular interactions between components.

Understanding solution behavior is essential for equilibrium calculations.

---

# Ideal Solutions

An ideal solution obeys Raoult's Law throughout the entire composition range.

Characteristics:

- Similar molecular interactions
- No excess properties
- Simple thermodynamic behavior

For ideal solutions:

$$
a_i=x_i
$$

and

$$
\gamma_i=1
$$

Examples:

- Benzene-Toluene

Ideal solutions are relatively rare in industrial practice.

---

# Non-Ideal Solutions

Most real solutions are non-ideal.

Causes include:

- Polarity differences
- Hydrogen bonding
- Molecular size differences

For non-ideal solutions:

$$
\gamma_i \neq 1
$$

Examples:

- Ethanol-Water
- Acetone-Water

These systems require activity coefficient models.

---

# Excess Properties

Excess properties measure deviation from ideal behavior.

General definition:

$$
M^E
=
M_{real}
-
M_{ideal}
$$

Examples:

- Excess Volume
- Excess Enthalpy
- Excess Gibbs Energy

Excess properties provide insight into molecular interactions.

---

# Activity

Activity represents the effective concentration of a component.

For a liquid solution:

$$
a_i
=
\gamma_i x_i
$$

Where:

- \(a_i\) = Activity
- \(x_i\) = Mole Fraction
- \(\gamma_i\) = Activity Coefficient

Activity is used extensively in equilibrium calculations.

---

# Activity Coefficient

The activity coefficient measures deviation from ideal solution behavior.

$$
\gamma_i
=
\frac{a_i}{x_i}
$$

For ideal solutions:

$$
\gamma_i=1
$$

For real solutions:

$$
\gamma_i\neq1
$$

Activity coefficients are essential in VLE calculations.

---

# Gibbs-Duhem Equation

The Gibbs-Duhem Equation relates the activity coefficients of components in a mixture.

At constant temperature and pressure:

$$
\sum x_i d\ln \gamma_i = 0
$$

Applications:

- Consistency testing
- Activity coefficient modeling
- Equilibrium calculations

It is one of the most important equations in solution thermodynamics.

# Chemical Reaction Equilibrium

Activities are used instead of concentrations in rigorous equilibrium calculations.

For a reaction:

$$
aA+bB
\rightleftharpoons
cC+dD
$$

The equilibrium constant becomes:

$$
K
=
\frac
{a_C^c a_D^d}
{a_A^a a_B^b}
$$

Activities therefore play a critical role in reaction thermodynamics.

# Solved Example

An ideal solution has:

$$
x_A=0.40
$$

For an ideal solution:

$$
\gamma_A=1
$$

Calculate activity.

Using:

$$
a_A=\gamma_A x_A
$$

Substituting:

$$
a_A=(1)(0.40)
$$

$$
a_A=0.40
$$

Therefore:

$$
a_A=0.40
$$

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Confuse activity and mole fraction
- Assume all mixtures are ideal
- Ignore activity coefficients
- Misinterpret excess properties
- Forget Gibbs-Duhem constraints

<WarningBox>
Mole fraction and activity are identical only for ideal solutions. Real solutions generally require activity coefficient corrections.
</WarningBox>
