# Introduction

Thermodynamic relations are mathematical equations that connect thermodynamic properties such as pressure, temperature, volume, entropy, enthalpy, and internal energy.

These relations allow engineers to:

- Derive property equations
- Estimate difficult-to-measure properties
- Analyze thermodynamic systems
- Predict phase behavior

Thermodynamic relations form the mathematical backbone of advanced thermodynamics.

---

# Why It Matters

Many thermodynamic properties cannot be measured directly.

Engineers often use thermodynamic relations to determine:

- Entropy changes
- Enthalpy changes
- Phase equilibrium properties
- Heat capacities
- Vapor pressure relationships

These relations are essential in:

- Process design
- Process simulation
- Thermodynamic modeling
- Chemical engineering calculations

---

# Core Concept

Thermodynamic relations are based on the fact that thermodynamic properties are interconnected.

Examples include:

- Pressure depends on temperature and volume
- Entropy depends on temperature and pressure
- Enthalpy depends on temperature and pressure

By applying calculus and thermodynamic laws, engineers can derive equations connecting these properties.

---

# Working Principle

The development of thermodynamic relations generally follows:

1. Start with fundamental thermodynamic equations.
2. Express properties as exact differentials.
3. Apply mathematical relationships.
4. Derive useful engineering equations.
5. Use the equations to estimate unknown properties.

This approach reduces experimental effort and improves predictive capability.

---

# Mathematical Definition

A thermodynamic property can often be expressed as:

$$
dz=Mdx+Ndy
$$

If:

$$
\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}
=
\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}
$$

then:

$$
dz
$$

is an exact differential.

This concept forms the basis of thermodynamic property relations.

---

# Exact Differentials

An exact differential corresponds to a thermodynamic property.

Examples:

$$
dU
$$

$$
dH
$$

$$
dS
$$

$$
dG
$$

Properties represented by exact differentials depend only on the initial and final states.

They do not depend on the path followed.

---

# State Functions

State functions depend only on the state of a system.

Examples:

- Internal Energy (\(U\))
- Enthalpy (\(H\))
- Entropy (\(S\))
- Gibbs Free Energy (\(G\))
- Pressure (\(P\))
- Temperature (\(T\))

For a state function:

$$
\Delta Property
=
Final - Initial
$$

Path followed is irrelevant.

---

# Path Functions

Path functions depend on the process path.

Examples:

- Heat (\(Q\))
- Work (\(W\))

For path functions:

$$
Q_{path1}
\neq
Q_{path2}
$$

Different paths can produce different values.

Therefore:

$$
\delta Q
$$

and

$$
\delta W
$$

are inexact differentials.

---

# Fundamental Property Relations

The First and Second Laws combine to form important property relations.

For a closed system:

$$
dU
=
TdS-PdV
$$

This equation is called the Fundamental Property Relation for Internal Energy.

Similarly:

$$
dH
=
TdS+VdP
$$

These equations form the starting point for advanced thermodynamic derivations.

---

# Maxwell Relations

Maxwell Relations are derived from the equality of mixed partial derivatives.

There are four important Maxwell relations.

First Maxwell Relation:

$$
\left(
\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}
\right)_S
=
-
\left(
\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}
\right)_V
$$

Second Maxwell Relation:

$$
\left(
\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}
\right)_S
=
\left(
\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}
\right)_P
$$

Third Maxwell Relation:

$$
\left(
\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}
\right)_V
=
\left(
\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}
\right)_T
$$

Fourth Maxwell Relation:

$$
\left(
\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}
\right)_P
=
-
\left(
\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}
\right)_T
$$

These relations are extensively used in property estimation.

---

# Gibbs Equations

The Gibbs equations connect entropy changes with measurable properties.

For a simple compressible system:

$$
TdS=dU+PdV
$$

and

$$
TdS=dH-VdP
$$

These equations are frequently used in entropy calculations.

---

# Clapeyron Equation

The Clapeyron Equation describes phase equilibrium.

$$
\frac{dP}{dT}
=
\frac{\Delta H}
{T\Delta V}
$$

Where:

- \(\Delta H\) = Latent Heat
- \(\Delta V\) = Volume Change

Applications:

- Vapor-liquid equilibrium
- Phase transitions
- Steam property calculations

---

# Clausius-Clapeyron Equation

A simplified form of the Clapeyron equation.

$$
\ln\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)
=
-\frac{\Delta H_{vap}}{R}
\left(
\frac{1}{T_2}
-
\frac{1}{T_1}
\right)
$$

Applications:

- Vapor pressure estimation
- Boiling point prediction
- Distillation calculations

This equation is one of the most widely used thermodynamic relations.

---

# Heat Capacity Relations

Heat capacities describe how much energy is required to change temperature.

At constant volume:

$$
C_v
=
\left(
\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}
\right)_V
$$

At constant pressure:

$$
C_p
=
\left(
\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}
\right)_P
$$

For ideal gases:

$$
C_p-C_v=R
$$

This is known as Mayer's Equation.

# Solved Example

An ideal gas has:

$$
C_p=29\;J/mol\cdot K
$$

Determine:

$$
C_v
$$

Using Mayer's Equation:

$$
C_p-C_v=R
$$

Substituting:

$$
29-C_v=8.314
$$

Therefore:

$$
C_v=20.686\;J/mol\cdot K
$$

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Confuse exact and inexact differentials
- Misapply Maxwell relations
- Use Celsius instead of Kelvin
- Forget thermodynamic assumptions
- Ignore units in property calculations

<WarningBox>
Heat and work are path functions, not state functions. This distinction is one of the most commonly tested thermodynamics concepts.
</WarningBox>
