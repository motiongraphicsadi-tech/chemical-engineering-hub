# Introduction

Fugacity is one of the most important concepts in advanced thermodynamics and phase equilibrium.

While pressure adequately describes ideal gas behavior, real fluids deviate significantly from ideality at high pressures and near critical conditions.

To account for these deviations, engineers use a corrected pressure-like property called **fugacity**.

Fugacity provides a more accurate measure of the tendency of a substance to escape from a phase.

It plays a central role in:

- Vapor-Liquid Equilibrium (VLE)
- Phase equilibrium calculations
- Equations of State
- Chemical potential calculations
- Process simulation

---

# Why It Matters

Real industrial systems rarely behave ideally.

Examples include:

- Natural gas processing
- Petroleum refining
- High-pressure reactors
- Refrigeration systems
- Supercritical fluid processes

Using pressure alone can produce significant errors.

Fugacity provides a thermodynamically rigorous method for:

- Predicting phase behavior
- Calculating equilibrium compositions
- Modeling real fluids
- Designing separation processes

---

# Core Concept

Pressure measures the mechanical force exerted by a fluid.

However, equilibrium behavior depends on the molecular tendency to escape from a phase.

This escaping tendency is represented by fugacity.

For an ideal gas:

$$
f=P
$$

For a real gas:

$$
f \neq P
$$

Thus fugacity acts as an effective pressure that accounts for non-ideal behavior.

---

# Working Principle

As pressure increases:

- Molecules interact more strongly.
- Attractive and repulsive forces become significant.
- Real gases deviate from ideal gas laws.

Fugacity corrects for these molecular interactions.

At low pressures:

$$
f \rightarrow P
$$

At high pressures:

$$
f \neq P
$$

The greater the deviation from ideality, the greater the difference between fugacity and pressure.

---

# Mathematical Definition

For a real gas:

$$
d\mu = RT\,d\ln f
$$

where:

- \( \mu \) = Chemical Potential
- \( f \) = Fugacity
- \( R \) = Gas Constant
- \( T \) = Temperature

This equation forms the thermodynamic basis of fugacity.

---

# Why Fugacity is Needed

Pressure alone is insufficient for describing:

- Real gas behavior
- High-pressure systems
- Phase equilibrium

For ideal gases:

$$
\mu = \mu^\circ + RT\ln P
$$

For real gases:

$$
\mu = \mu^\circ + RT\ln f
$$

Replacing pressure with fugacity allows the same mathematical framework to be applied to real systems.

---

# Concept of Escaping Tendency

Fugacity is often described as the escaping tendency of a substance.

A substance with higher fugacity has a greater tendency to leave its current phase.

Examples:

- Vapor has higher escaping tendency than liquid.
- High-pressure gases have greater escaping tendency.

At equilibrium:

Escaping tendencies become equal.

This leads to:

$$
f^V = f^L
$$

which is the fundamental condition for phase equilibrium.

---

# Fugacity Definition

Fugacity is defined as the pressure that an ideal gas would need to have in order to possess the same chemical potential as the real fluid.

Mathematically:

$$
\mu = \mu^\circ + RT\ln f
$$

Fugacity therefore serves as a corrected pressure term for real fluids.

---

# Fugacity Coefficient

The fugacity coefficient measures deviation from ideal behavior.

It is defined as:

$$
\phi = \frac{f}{P}
$$

Where:

- \( \phi \) = Fugacity Coefficient
- \( f \) = Fugacity
- \( P \) = Pressure

For an ideal gas:

$$
\phi = 1
$$

For real gases:

$$
\phi \neq 1
$$

---

# Pure Component Fugacity

For a pure fluid:

$$
f = \phi P
$$

At low pressures:

$$
\phi \approx 1
$$

Therefore:

$$
f \approx P
$$

As pressure increases, fugacity increasingly deviates from pressure.

---

# Mixture Fugacity

For component \(i\) in a mixture:

$$
f_i = y_i \phi_i P
$$

Where:

- \(y_i\) = Vapor Mole Fraction
- \(\phi_i\) = Fugacity Coefficient
- \(P\) = Total Pressure

Mixture fugacities are essential for:

- VLE calculations
- Flash calculations
- Distillation design

---

# Relation with EOS

Fugacity coefficients are commonly obtained using Equations of State (EOS).

Examples:

- Van der Waals EOS
- Redlich-Kwong EOS
- Soave-Redlich-Kwong EOS
- Peng-Robinson EOS

EOS models allow engineers to calculate:

$$
\phi
$$

and subsequently:

$$
f
$$

for real fluids.

---

# Applications in Phase Equilibrium

Fugacity is extensively used in phase equilibrium calculations.

At equilibrium:

$$
f_i^V=f_i^L
$$

This criterion is applied in:

- Vapor-Liquid Equilibrium
- Liquid-Liquid Equilibrium
- Vapor-Solid Equilibrium
- Flash calculations

Modern process simulators use fugacity-based methods extensively.

# Solved Example

A gas exists at:

$$
P = 50\;bar
$$

with fugacity coefficient:

$$
\phi = 0.92
$$

Calculate fugacity.

Using:

$$
f = \phi P
$$

Substituting:

$$
f = 0.92 \times 50
$$

$$
f = 46\;bar
$$

Therefore:

$$
f = 46\;bar
$$

---

# Common Mistakes

Students commonly:

- Assume fugacity equals pressure under all conditions
- Confuse fugacity with fugacity coefficient
- Forget that fugacity approaches pressure only at low pressure
- Ignore EOS dependence
- Misapply fugacity in ideal systems

<WarningBox>
Fugacity equals pressure only for ideal gases. Real fluids generally require fugacity corrections.
</WarningBox>

# Summary

Fugacity is a thermodynamic property that represents the escaping tendency of a real fluid and serves as a corrected pressure for non-ideal systems.

By introducing fugacity and fugacity coefficients, engineers can accurately model real-fluid behavior, predict phase equilibrium, and perform reliable VLE calculations.

Fugacity is one of the most important concepts in advanced thermodynamics and chemical process simulation.
