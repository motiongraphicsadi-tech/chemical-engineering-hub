# Introduction

Process modeling is the mathematical representation of a physical process.

Instead of directly studying a real plant, engineers develop mathematical models that describe how process variables change with time.

Process models help engineers:

- Predict process behavior
- Design control systems
- Perform simulations
- Troubleshoot operations
- Optimize plant performance

Process modeling forms the foundation of process dynamics and control engineering.

---

# Why It Matters

Industrial plants contain thousands of interacting processes.

Examples include:

- Storage tanks
- Heat exchangers
- Distillation columns
- Reactors
- Mixing vessels

Testing every operating condition on a real plant is expensive and risky.

Process models allow engineers to:

- Study system behavior safely
- Design controllers
- Analyze disturbances
- Improve process performance

Modern process control is impossible without process modeling.

---

# Core Concept

A process model describes the relationship between:

- Input Variables
- Output Variables
- Disturbances

General representation:

$$
Output = f(Input)
$$

In process control, models are often expressed using:

- Differential Equations
- Transfer Functions
- State Space Models

The goal is to predict how the process responds to changes in input.

---

# Working Principle

Process modeling generally follows these steps:

1. Define system boundaries.
2. Identify inputs and outputs.
3. Apply conservation laws.
4. Develop differential equations.
5. Obtain transfer functions.
6. Validate with plant data.

The resulting model can then be used for analysis and controller design.

---

# Mathematical Definition

Most process models are developed using conservation principles.

General balance equation:

$$
Accumulation = Input - Output + Generation - Consumption
$$

For many tank systems:

$$
Accumulation = Input - Output
$$

This balance becomes the basis for deriving dynamic models.

---

# Tank Systems

Tank systems are among the most important examples in process control.

Typical variables include:

- Inlet Flow Rate
- Outlet Flow Rate
- Liquid Level

Input:

$$
q_{in}
$$

Output:

$$
h
$$

Where:

- $q_{in}$ = Inlet flow rate
- $h$ = Liquid level

Tank systems are commonly modeled as first order processes.

---

# Non-Interacting Tank System

A non-interacting tank system consists of two or more tanks connected such that the flow leaving one tank is independent of the liquid level in the next tank.

Characteristics:

- Individual tank dynamics remain independent.
- Easier mathematical analysis.
- Transfer functions can be multiplied directly.

For two tanks:

$$
G(s)
=
\frac{K_1}
{\tau_1 s + 1}
\cdot
\frac{K_2}
{\tau_2 s + 1}
$$

Characteristics:

- Second order behavior
- No hydraulic interaction
- Simpler modeling

---

# Interacting Tank System

In an interacting tank system, the flow between tanks depends on the level difference between the tanks.

Characteristics:

- Tanks influence each other.
- Dynamics become more complex.
- Strong coupling exists.

The flow between tanks is often represented as:

$$
q
=
\frac{h_1-h_2}
{R}
$$

Where:

- $h_1$ = Level in Tank 1
- $h_2$ = Level in Tank 2
- $R$ = Hydraulic Resistance

Interacting systems generally produce slower and more complex responses than non-interacting systems.

# Storage Tanks

Storage tanks are commonly modeled to predict level variations.

Applications:

- Raw material storage
- Product storage
- Utility storage

Important objectives:

- Prevent overflow
- Prevent dry running
- Maintain inventory levels

Storage tanks are often modeled as first order systems.

---

# Mixing Systems

Mixing systems involve:

- Material accumulation
- Composition changes
- Dynamic mixing behavior

Variables commonly modeled include:

- Concentration
- Flow rate
- Volume

Mixing tank models are extensively used in chemical engineering.

---

# Surge Tanks

Surge tanks are used to absorb process disturbances.

Functions:

- Reduce flow fluctuations
- Stabilize downstream processes
- Improve plant operation

Because surge tanks provide temporary storage, they naturally exhibit dynamic behavior that must be modeled.

---

# Transfer Function Representation

After deriving differential equations, Laplace Transform is applied.

Example first order transfer function:

$$
G(s)
=
\frac{K}
{\tau s + 1}
$$

For interacting and non-interacting tanks, higher order transfer functions may be obtained.

Transfer functions simplify controller design and stability analysis.

# Solved Example

A storage tank has:

$$
q_{in}=5 \; m^3/hr
$$

and

$$
q_{out}=3 \; m^3/hr
$$

Using:

$$
Accumulation = Input - Output
$$

Therefore:

$$
Accumulation = 5 - 3
$$

$$
Accumulation = 2 \; m^3/hr
$$

The liquid volume inside the tank increases at:

$$
2 \; m^3/hr
$$

# Common Mistakes

Students frequently:

- Ignore system boundaries
- Confuse interacting and non-interacting tanks
- Forget accumulation terms
- Use steady-state equations for dynamic systems
- Ignore model assumptions

<WarningBox>
A model is only as accurate as its assumptions. Always verify process models using real plant data.
</WarningBox>

# Summary

Process modeling is the foundation of process control engineering. It converts physical processes into mathematical models that can be analyzed, simulated, and controlled.

Tank systems provide the most common modeling examples, while interacting and non-interacting tanks demonstrate how system structure affects dynamic behavior.

Accurate process models help engineers design controllers, optimize operations, improve safety, and better understand industrial processes.
