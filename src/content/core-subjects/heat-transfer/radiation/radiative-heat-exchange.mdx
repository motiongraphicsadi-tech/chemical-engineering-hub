# Radiative Heat Exchange

<TopicRoadmap />


## How Does Heat Transfer Between a Hot Wall and a Cold Wall?

Consider two surfaces facing each other:

```text
Hot Wall                    Cold Wall

T₁                           T₂

███████  →→→→→→→→→→  ███████
```

The hot wall emits radiation toward the cold wall.

At the same time, the cold wall also emits radiation toward the hot wall.

However:

- The hotter surface emits more radiation.
- The colder surface emits less radiation.

The difference between these two radiation streams produces a **net heat transfer** from hot to cold.

---

# Net Radiative Heat Transfer

For two surfaces exchanging radiation:

$$
q = \varepsilon \sigma A
\left(
T_1^4 - T_2^4
\right)
$$

Where:

| Symbol | Description |
|----------|----------|
| \(q\) | Net radiation heat transfer rate (W) |
| \(\varepsilon\) | Emissivity |
| \(\sigma\) | Stefan-Boltzmann Constant |
| \(A\) | Surface Area (m²) |
| \(T_1\) | Hot Surface Temperature (K) |
| \(T_2\) | Cold Surface Temperature (K) |

---

# Understanding the Equation

Notice the term:

$$
T_1^4 - T_2^4
$$

Heat transfer depends on the difference of the fourth powers of temperature.

This means:

- Small temperature increases can produce large radiation changes.
- Radiation becomes extremely important at high temperatures.

---

# Visualization

```text
Hot Surface (T₁)

↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑↑
Radiation Emitted

      ↓ Net Heat Flow ↓

↓↓↓↓
Radiation Received

Cold Surface (T₂)
```

Since the hot surface emits more radiation than it receives:

Net heat flows from hot to cold.

---

# Special Case

If:

$$
T_1 = T_2
$$

Then:

$$
q = 0
$$

Both surfaces emit equal radiation.

Therefore no net heat transfer occurs.

---

# Practical Example: Furnace Wall → Process Tube

Inside a furnace:

```text
Hot Refractory Wall
        ↓ Radiation
Process Tube
```

The refractory wall emits large amounts of thermal radiation.

The process tube absorbs this energy and its temperature increases.

In many furnaces, radiative heat transfer is the dominant heating mechanism.

---

# Practical Example: Reactor → Surroundings

A hot reactor continuously exchanges radiation with its surroundings.

```text
Hot Reactor
      ↓
Radiation
      ↓
Atmosphere
```

Even without direct contact, the reactor loses energy through radiation.

This heat loss becomes significant at elevated temperatures.

---

# Practical Example: Steam Pipe

An uninsulated steam pipe emits radiation to nearby surfaces and the surrounding atmosphere.

As surface temperature increases:

- Radiation loss increases rapidly.
- Energy efficiency decreases.

This is one reason insulation is widely used.

---

# Why Radiative Heat Exchange Matters

Engineers use radiative heat exchange calculations for:

- Furnace design
- Fired heaters
- Boilers
- Reactor heat loss calculations
- Radiation shields
- Insulation analysis
- Flare radiation studies

Ignoring radiation can lead to large errors in high-temperature systems.

---

# Engineering Perspective

When analyzing a hot piece of equipment, always ask:

- What is the surrounding temperature?
- What surfaces are exchanging radiation?
- Is radiation significant compared to convection?

At high temperatures, radiative heat exchange often becomes the dominant heat transfer mechanism.

# Key Takeaways

- All surfaces emit thermal radiation.
- Net heat transfer occurs because hotter surfaces emit more radiation than colder surfaces.
- Radiative heat exchange is given by:

$$
q = \varepsilon \sigma A (T_1^4 - T_2^4)
$$

- Heat always flows from higher temperature to lower temperature.
- Radiation becomes increasingly important as temperature increases.
- Furnace walls, reactor shells, and hot pipes continuously exchange radiation with their surroundings.

---

# Summary

Radiative heat exchange occurs whenever two surfaces at different temperatures exchange thermal radiation. Both surfaces emit radiation, but the hotter surface emits more, resulting in a net heat transfer from hot to cold. The Stefan-Boltzmann relationship shows that radiation depends on the difference between the fourth powers of temperature, making radiation especially important in furnaces, reactors, boilers, and other high-temperature industrial equipment.
